# The Deal Desk, episode 21: Week three review, the D-C-F from cash flow to share price
Six episodes, one café chain, one chain of sums from EBITDA to a price per share. Then Crédit Agricole buys a German bank from Lone Star, and nobody says what it paid.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-one. Review day. Week three built the D-C-F: free cash flow, the cost of capital, terminal value, sensitivities, levered cash flow and, yesterday, banks. Today I take Sofia through it from start to finish, no notes. If you're listening, answer before she does.

SOFIA: No notes. Fine.

MARCO: We start with the story that sums up the week. D-F-C Global, the payday lender. Lone Star, the private equity firm, bought it for nine dollars fifty a share, and shareholders went to court. When a correction threatened to push the court's D-C-F below the deal price, the judge changed one number, long-term growth, from three point one percent to four. Fair value came out at ten dollars thirty. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed it in August twenty seventeen. Why can less than one point of growth move a valuation that far?

SOFIA: Because growth sits in the terminal value, and the terminal value is most of a D-C-F. And it's at the bottom of the fraction: discount rate minus growth. Shrink that gap and the value jumps.

MARCO: Good. Keep Lone Star in mind, because it's back in today's deal of the day. Now the week's other stories, one line each. Klarna?

SOFIA: Down eighty-five percent in thirteen months. The business was fine. The discount rate went up.

MARCO: Tibco?

SOFIA: Shares counted twice. About a hundred million dollars lost in the plumbing.

MARCO: Thames Water and Credit Suisse?

SOFIA: Thames: the cash paid the lenders before the owners. Credit Suisse: sold for a fifteenth of its book, because nobody trusted the book.

MARCO: Different stories, one model. Round one, free cash flow. Cafe Group. EBITDA one hundred and thirty, depreciation and amortisation thirty, tax twenty-five percent, capex forty, working capital up fifteen. Unlevered free cash flow?

SOFIA: EBIT one hundred. Tax it, ignoring interest: seventy-five. Add back the thirty, take off forty of capex and fifteen of working capital. Fifty.

MARCO: Clean. Next year, working capital falls by ten instead. What does that do to cash?

SOFIA: Working capital moving again. Cash goes down by ten.

MARCO: First trap, upside down. When working capital falls, customers are paying faster or stock is running down, and cash comes out of the business. Free cash flow goes up by ten. Round two, the discount rate. Risk-free rate four percent, beta one point two, equity risk premium five percent.

SOFIA: Four plus one point two times five. Cost of equity, ten percent.

MARCO: Cafe Group borrows at eight percent. At market value it's three quarters equity, one quarter debt. Weighted average cost of capital?

SOFIA: Three quarters of ten is seven and a half. A quarter of eight is two. Nine and a half percent.

MARCO: Second trap. The tax. Interest cuts the tax bill, so debt at eight percent costs six after tax. A quarter of six is one and a half. Nine percent. The tax saving lives in the rate, which is exactly why we left interest out of the cash flow.

SOFIA: Half a point. Does it matter?

MARCO: Ask D-F-C Global. Round three, terminal value. Final-year cash flow one hundred, growth two percent, discount rate nine percent.

SOFIA: Next year's cash flow, so one hundred and two, over seven percent. About one thousand four hundred and sixty. That's a value at the end of year five, so I divide by about one point five four. Roughly nine hundred and fifty today.

MARCO: Both traps avoided: next year's cash flow, and discounted back. Add the five forecast years, just under four hundred in today's money. Enterprise value?

SOFIA: About one thousand three hundred and forty. Seventy percent of it from the terminal value.

MARCO: Round four, the share price. Millions of euros. Net debt three hundred and forty, one hundred million shares, and staff options and restricted shares worth another five million.

SOFIA: One thousand three hundred and forty minus three hundred and forty. One billion of equity, over one hundred million shares. Ten euros.

MARCO: Third trap, the Tibco trap. Divide by the diluted count, one hundred and five million. About nine euros fifty. Then a sensitivity table around it, because a D-C-F is a range. Round five, the shareholders' slice. Unlevered fifty, interest twenty, ten of debt repaid.

SOFIA: Interest after tax is fifteen. Fifty minus fifteen minus ten: twenty-five. Discount it at the cost of equity, ten percent, not the nine percent wack. Then take off net debt to get to equity value.

MARCO: Fourth trap. A levered D-C-F already gives you equity value. The interest and repayments are inside the cash flows, so taking off net debt again charges the shareholders twice. Last round, banks. Return on equity twelve percent, cost of equity ten, growth two. Price to book?

SOFIA: Twelve minus two, over ten minus two. One point two five. And no E-V to EBITDA, because deposits are the raw material, not the funding.

MARCO: Good. So where did you slip?

SOFIA: Which way working capital moves cash. The tax on debt. The share count. Whether the debt was already gone.

MARCO: And one rule under all of it. Unlevered cash, at the weighted average cost of capital, gives enterprise value. Levered cash, at the cost of equity, gives equity value. Then divide by the diluted shares.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First, and you will get this one: walk me through a D-C-F.

SOFIA: Forecast the free cash flows, discount them at the wack, add a terminal value, and that's the value of the company.

MARCO: Right start, missing ending, and value of the company is vague. The strong answer: project unlevered free cash flow for five to ten years: EBIT after tax, plus depreciation, minus capex and the increase in working capital. Calculate a terminal value by perpetuity growth or an exit multiple, and cross-check one with the other. Discount the forecast years and the terminal value at the weighted average cost of capital. That's enterprise value. Subtract net debt, divide by diluted shares, and show a range.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Interest rates rise by two points. What happens to your D-C-F, and which companies suffer most?

SOFIA: The cost of debt goes up, so the wack goes up, and every company falls by about the same amount.

MARCO: First half right, second half wrong. The risk-free rate rises, so the cost of equity rises too. And the damage isn't even. Cash a year away barely moves. Cash ten years away, and the terminal value behind it, takes the full hit. Mature companies paying cash today hold up; growth companies whose profits sit years out fall hardest. That's Klarna.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A French bank buys a German one, and the seller is our friend from the cold open. On Monday, the twelfth of October, Crédit Agricole agreed to buy all of IKB Deutsche Industriebank from Lone Star. IKB is based in Düsseldorf, was founded in nineteen twenty-four, and lends to Germany's Mittelstand, its mid-sized companies.

SOFIA: How big is it?

MARCO: About six hundred employees. At the end of twenty twenty-five, total assets of fourteen point three billion euros and a loan book of eight point two billion. Net profit for twenty twenty-five: sixty-one million euros. Lone Star bought it in two thousand and eight, after losses on American subprime mortgages had forced a rescue.

SOFIA: And the price?

MARCO: Not disclosed. Crédit Agricole says it's paying from its own resources, the return is consistent with its targets, and the deal will cut its core capital ratio, C-E-T-one, by about twenty basis points at closing. Completion is expected by the end of the second quarter of twenty twenty-seven, subject to the European Central Bank and competition approvals. Bloomberg reported that Helaba, a German regional bank, had considered a binding offer of more than six hundred million euros, and won't be buying.

SOFIA: So if the price were six hundred million, that's six hundred over sixty-one. About ten times earnings.

MARCO: Nicely done, as long as you keep the if. Here's your interview question. The price isn't public. How would you judge whether Crédit Agricole paid a fair one?

SOFIA: Build a D-C-F of IKB's free cash flow, discount it at its wack, and compare.

MARCO: That's yesterday's first trap. A bank's deposits and debt are its raw material, so there's no meaningful enterprise value or wack. Value the equity. Price to tangible book, set against IKB's return on tangible equity. Price to earnings, with that sixty-one million as a check. A dividend discount model, with payouts limited by capital. Then the buyer's side: those twenty basis points are capital Crédit Agricole is spending, and the deal works if the return on it beats the cost of equity.

SOFIA: So for a bank, part of the price is the capital it uses up.

MARCO: Exactly. Quiz. For you listening. Question one: EBIT is two hundred, the tax rate is twenty-five percent, depreciation is forty, capex is sixty, and working capital falls by twenty. What's unlevered free cash flow? Question two: equity is eighty percent of the company at market value and costs ten percent; debt is twenty percent and costs five percent before tax; the tax rate is twenty percent. What's the weighted average cost of capital? Question three: final-year free cash flow is one hundred, long-term growth is two percent, and the discount rate is eight percent. What's the terminal value? Question four: a levered D-C-F gives nine hundred million, net debt is three hundred million, and there are eighty million basic and ninety million diluted shares. What's a share worth? Question five: if a buyer paid six hundred million euros for IKB, which earned sixty-one million last year, roughly what price to earnings is that?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: one hundred and fifty. One hundred and fifty after tax, plus forty, minus sixty, plus twenty released from working capital. Two: eight point eight percent. Eight from the equity, plus twenty percent of four, the after-tax cost of debt. Three: one thousand seven hundred. One hundred and two over six percent. Four: ten. Nine hundred over ninety diluted shares; the debt is already inside the cash flows. Five: about ten times.

SOFIA: A week ago I'd have called EBITDA cash. Now I can get from cash to a share price.

MARCO: Which is what a buyer does, before asking the harder question: what are the two businesses worth together? Tomorrow, episode twenty-two: synergies. Anheuser-Busch InBev's takeover of SABMiller, the savings it promised, and how much of them a buyer should pay away.
