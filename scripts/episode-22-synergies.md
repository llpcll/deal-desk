# The Deal Desk, episode 22: Synergies, or the beer merger that ran on savings
Ten years ago this week, Anheuser-Busch InBev closed its takeover of SABMiller on a promise of one point four billion dollars a year. Then the Weston family buys Boots, and nobody names a synergy.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-two. So far we've valued companies on their own. Today, two companies together, and the extra value a buyer says it can create by combining them. Synergies. And we start ten years ago this week, with beer.

SOFIA: My favourite kind of case study.

MARCO: On the eleventh of November twenty fifteen, Anheuser-Busch InBev, the world's largest brewer, agreed to buy the second largest, SABMiller, the London-listed owner of Peroni and Grolsch. The price: forty-four pounds a share in cash, about seventy-one billion pounds for the whole company.

SOFIA: And what was SABMiller trading at before?

MARCO: Twenty-nine pounds thirty-four on the fourteenth of September, the last day before talk of an approach. So a premium of about fifty percent.

SOFIA: Fifty percent. Why would anyone pay that?

MARCO: That's today's question. The answer in the announcement was cost synergies: at least one point four billion dollars a year of savings before tax, at the full rate by the end of the fourth year. And it said where they'd come from. About thirty-five percent from corporate and overlapping regional head offices, a quarter from breweries and distribution, a fifth from procurement and engineering, and a fifth from sharing best practice.

SOFIA: Two head offices, and you only need one.

MARCO: Exactly. Getting there would cost about nine hundred million dollars, once. And one more line to remember. Revenue synergies might come too, it said, but they couldn't be quantified.

SOFIA: Then what happened?

MARCO: Brexit. After the vote in June twenty sixteen the pound fell, and forty-four pounds looked less attractive to SABMiller's shareholders. In July, A-B InBev raised the offer to forty-five pounds, valuing SABMiller at about seventy-nine billion. The deal completed on the tenth of October twenty sixteen.

SOFIA: And did the savings arrive?

MARCO: The target grew. Add the one point oh five billion dollars of savings SABMiller had already planned on its own, and you had two point four five billion. In March twenty seventeen, A-B InBev raised it to two point eight billion, with eight hundred and twenty-nine million already delivered.

SOFIA: So the buyer was right, and the fifty percent was worth it.

MARCO: Not so fast. Delivering savings and paying the right price for them are two different questions. Fifty percent of an undisturbed value of about forty-seven billion pounds is a premium of more than twenty billion. Take tax off one point four billion dollars a year and put ten times on it: around ten billion dollars. On the published numbers, the premium was bigger than the synergies.

MARCO: So start with the definition. A synergy is the value of the two businesses together, minus the value of each on its own. Two kinds. Cost synergies: one head office instead of two, fewer breweries, better terms from suppliers. Revenue synergies: selling more, by pushing one company's products through the other's customers, or charging more.

SOFIA: Revenue synergies sound better. Growth beats cutting costs.

MARCO: First trap. Bigger on the slide, smaller in the model. A cost saving is in the buyer's own hands: it closes the office, it signs the new supply contract. A revenue synergy needs customers to behave differently, and competitors to sit still. That's why A-B InBev only put a number on costs. Bankers discount revenue synergies heavily, and many buyers leave them out of the price.

SOFIA: Fine. So how do I value them?

MARCO: Worked example, round numbers, millions of euros. Hop Brewing wants to buy Malt Brewing. On its own, before any bid, Malt is worth one thousand. Hop finds cost savings of one hundred a year, before tax. What are they worth?

SOFIA: One hundred a year, forever, at a ten percent discount rate. One hundred over ten percent: one thousand. The savings are worth as much as the whole company.

MARCO: Second trap. Tax. Saving one hundred of cost adds one hundred to profit, and the taxman takes his share. At twenty-five percent, Hop keeps seventy-five. Seventy-five over ten percent: seven hundred and fifty.

SOFIA: Seven hundred and fifty, then.

MARCO: Not yet. Savings cost money to get: redundancy payments, closing a brewery, merging the computer systems. Say one-off costs of one hundred, after tax. And the savings don't arrive on day one. If they take a year to build up, the wait costs about forty in today's money.

SOFIA: Seven hundred and fifty, minus one hundred, minus forty. Six hundred and ten.

MARCO: Call it six hundred. That's the net present value of the synergies. So what's the most Hop should pay for Malt?

SOFIA: One thousand plus six hundred. One thousand six hundred.

MARCO: Right. That's the walk-away price. Pay it, and every euro of synergy goes to Malt's shareholders. Hop's own shareholders get the work, the risk, and nothing extra.

SOFIA: So Hop should offer less.

MARCO: Much less, ideally. Say Hop offers one thousand three hundred: a thirty percent premium. Of the six hundred of synergies, three hundred goes to the sellers as premium, and three hundred stays with Hop. The question bankers ask is: how much of the synergies are we paying away? Here, half. And if another brewer bids, that share rises, because the winner tends to be whoever is most optimistic about the savings.

SOFIA: Now revenue synergies. Hop sells two hundred more beer a year through Malt's pubs. Same maths: after tax one hundred and fifty, over ten percent, one thousand five hundred.

MARCO: Third trap. Two hundred of sales isn't two hundred of profit. You have to brew the beer, ship it and sell it. At a twenty percent operating margin, two hundred of revenue is forty of profit before tax. Thirty after tax. Three hundred of value, before anyone applies a haircut for doubt.

SOFIA: A fifth of what I said.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what are synergies, and which kind do you trust more?

SOFIA: The extra value from putting two companies together. Revenue synergies, because they're usually bigger.

MARCO: The definition's fine; the preference is backwards. Strong answer: synergies are the value of the combined business above the two standalone values. Cost synergies, like overlapping head offices and purchasing, are more reliable because the buyer controls them. Revenue synergies depend on customers and competitors, so they're discounted heavily. Value either kind after tax, net of the one-off costs, and allow for the time they take to arrive.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: A buyer expects synergies with a net present value of six hundred million. How much of that should it pay to the seller?

SOFIA: Up to six hundred. If it doesn't, someone else will.

MARCO: Six hundred is the walk-away price, not the plan. Strong answer: standalone value plus net synergies is the ceiling. Share as little as the competition allows, because the buyer's shareholders carry the risk of integration. Then test it: what share of the synergies does the premium give away, and does the deal still create value if only half the savings come through?

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A British high-street name changes hands. Last Wednesday, the seventh of October, Wittington Investments, the holding company of the Canadian branch of the Weston family, agreed to buy Boots. Total price, about eight point nine billion dollars, including the debt it takes on. Roughly six point seven billion pounds.

SOFIA: Boots the chemist?

MARCO: The same. Based in Nottingham, more than one hundred and seventy years old, over one thousand eight hundred stores and more than fifty thousand staff. The deal includes Boots Opticians and the No Seven Beauty Company. The sellers are Sycamore Partners, the American private equity firm that took Walgreens Boots Alliance private last year, and the family of Stefano Pessina.

SOFIA: And the Westons are paying on their own?

MARCO: No. Fairfax Financial, the Toronto insurance group run by Prem Watsa, is committing up to about two point three billion dollars and expects to own half the equity. Wittington runs the business, and Galen Weston becomes chairman. Completion is expected in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven, subject to approvals.

SOFIA: So the total is eight point nine and Fairfax puts in two point three. The debt is the difference: six point six billion.

MARCO: Careful. Two point three buys half. So the equity is up to about four point six, and on that rough reading the rest, about four point three billion, is debt. And it's "up to", so treat it as a ceiling, not a fact.

SOFIA: And the synergies? We've just spent ten minutes on them.

MARCO: The announcement doesn't give a figure. Wittington has no British pharmacy chain, so there's no second head office to close: A-B InBev's biggest bucket simply isn't there. What the Westons do have is Loblaw, which owns Shoppers Drug Mart, Canada's largest pharmacy, health and beauty business. They talk about upgrading the stores, the online shop and health services.

SOFIA: So the synergy is knowing how to run a pharmacy.

MARCO: Sharing best practice. A fifth of A-B InBev's list, and the hardest kind to put a number on. Here's your interview question. With no synergy figure, how do you judge whether eight point nine billion dollars is a fair price?

SOFIA: Treat the synergies as zero and value Boots on its own.

MARCO: Right start, half the answer. Value Boots on its own: E-V to EBITDA against other pharmacy and beauty retailers, past deals in the sector, and a D-C-F where the store upgrades show up as capex. Then ask what this owner adds: know-how from Shoppers, and patient money. A family owner may accept a lower return than a fund that has to sell in five years. That isn't a textbook synergy, but it's why one buyer can pay more than another.

SOFIA: So the best buyer isn't always the one with the most savings.

MARCO: Nicely put. Quiz. For you listening. Question one: a buyer finds cost savings of one hundred a year before tax; tax is twenty-five percent, the discount rate ten percent, and there's no growth. What are the savings worth? Question two: a target is worth one thousand on its own, and the net synergies are worth six hundred. What's the walk-away price? Question three: why do bankers trust cost synergies more than revenue synergies? Question four: what was the largest source of A-B InBev's promised one point four billion dollars of savings? Question five: Fairfax commits up to two point three billion dollars for half of Boots' equity, in a deal worth eight point nine billion including debt. Roughly how much is debt?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: seven hundred and fifty. Seventy-five after tax, over ten percent. Two: one thousand six hundred. Pay that, and the sellers get all the synergies. Three: because the buyer controls them; revenue synergies depend on customers and competitors. Four: corporate and overlapping regional head offices, about thirty-five percent. Five: about four point three billion dollars. Two point three is half the equity, so the equity is about four point six.

SOFIA: So synergies are a promise, and the price decides who gets paid for keeping it.

MARCO: And before anyone gets paid, there's a process: approach, due diligence, signing, closing. Tomorrow, episode twenty-three: how a deal process works, and what happens when the buyer tries to walk away. Elon Musk and Twitter.
