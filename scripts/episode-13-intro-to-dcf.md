# The Deal Desk, episode 13: The D-C-F, and why a euro today beats a euro tomorrow
Two experts valued Dell on the same day with the same method. One said twenty-eight dollars a share, the other under thirteen. Welcome to the discounted cash flow.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode thirteen. For three episodes we've valued companies by comparing them with other companies. Today, a different idea: value a business on its own, from the cash it will produce. That's the discounted cash flow, or D-C-F. And it starts in a courtroom.

SOFIA: A courtroom?

MARCO: In Delaware. In twenty thirteen, Michael Dell, the founder, and the investment firm Silver Lake took Dell private in a deal worth about twenty-five billion dollars. Thirteen dollars seventy-five a share. Some shareholders thought that was too low. Under Delaware law, they could refuse the price and ask a judge to set what's called fair value.

SOFIA: And how does a judge decide what a company is worth?

MARCO: With experts. Each side brought one, and both used a D-C-F. The shareholders' expert said Dell was worth twenty-eight dollars sixty-one a share. The company's expert said twelve dollars sixty-eight.

SOFIA: Same company, same method, and one number is more than double the other?

MARCO: Across all the shares, the gap was about twenty-eight billion dollars. Bigger than the deal itself. On the thirty-first of May, twenty sixteen, the judge rejected both, built his own D-C-F, and arrived at seventeen dollars sixty-two. About twenty-eight percent above the deal price.

SOFIA: So the shareholders won.

MARCO: For a while. In December twenty seventeen, the Delaware Supreme Court reversed it. It said the judge had been wrong to give no weight to the market: the share price and the deal price deserved heavy weight. Keep that story in mind. The D-C-F is the most logical valuation method there is, and the easiest to bend. By the end of today you'll see why.

SOFIA: Okay. Where does it start?

MARCO: With one question. Would you rather have a hundred euros today, or a hundred euros in a year?

SOFIA: Today. Obviously.

MARCO: Why?

SOFIA: Inflation. In a year, a hundred euros buys less.

MARCO: That's one reason. There are two more. Opportunity cost: if you have the money today, you can invest it and earn a return. And risk: a promise of money in a year might not be kept. Put those together and you get the time value of money. A euro today is worth more than a euro tomorrow.

SOFIA: How much more?

MARCO: That depends on the return you could earn. Say ten percent a year. A hundred euros today becomes a hundred and ten in a year. Leave it another year and you earn ten percent on a hundred and ten, so you have a hundred and twenty-one. That's compounding: you earn returns on your returns.

SOFIA: That's future value. Where's the discounting?

MARCO: Run it backwards. If a hundred and ten euros in a year is worth the same to you as a hundred today, then the present value of that hundred and ten is a hundred. You divide by one plus the rate. For money in two years, you divide twice. In general: present value equals the cash flow, divided by one plus the discount rate, raised to the power of the number of years.

SOFIA: Let me try. A hundred euros in one year at ten percent. Ten percent of a hundred is ten, so it's worth ninety today.

MARCO: First trap. Close, but no. You subtracted ten percent. Discounting means dividing by one point one. A hundred divided by one point one is about ninety-one. Check it: ninety-one invested at ten percent grows back to a hundred. Ninety would only grow to ninety-nine.

SOFIA: And in two years I'd subtract twenty percent and get eighty?

MARCO: Same trap, bigger. Divide by one point one twice, which means dividing by one point two one. About eighty-three. The gap grows the further out you go, because compounding works in both directions.

SOFIA: Okay. So how does that value a company?

MARCO: Our café chain again. Imagine it will hand its owners a hundred of cash in each of the next three years, and then close. Discount rate ten percent. Year one: about ninety-one. Year two: about eighty-three. Year three: a hundred divided by one point three three one, about seventy-five.

SOFIA: Ninety-one plus eighty-three plus seventy-five. Two hundred and forty-nine.

MARCO: So three hundred of future cash is worth about two hundred and forty-nine today. That is a D-C-F. Every version you'll ever build is the same idea with more detail.

SOFIA: What detail?

MARCO: Four pieces. One: forecast the company's free cash flow, usually for five to ten years. Episode fifteen is all about which cash flow. Two: a company doesn't close after year five, so you add a terminal value for everything after the forecast. That's episode seventeen. Three: discount all of it at the right rate, normally the weighted average cost of capital, which is episode sixteen. Four: add it up. That gives you enterprise value, and from there you bridge to equity value, exactly as in episode nine.

SOFIA: So the discount rate is ten percent for every company?

MARCO: Second trap. Ten was just for our example. The rate reflects risk: the riskier the cash flows, the higher the return investors demand, and the higher the rate. A water utility is usually discounted at a lower rate than a young software company.

SOFIA: And a higher rate means a higher value, because investors want more.

MARCO: Third trap, and people say it in interviews all the time. It's the opposite. A higher discount rate means you divide by a bigger number, so the present value falls. Investors demand more return, so they pay less today for the same future cash. Try it: our café at fifteen percent instead of ten is worth about two hundred and twenty-eight, not two hundred and forty-nine.

SOFIA: So back to Dell. How did two experts get to twenty-eight dollars and twelve dollars?

MARCO: Every input is a judgement. How fast will sales grow? What margins? What discount rate? What happens after the forecast? Small changes, compounded over many years, move the answer a lot. And in most D-C-Fs, the terminal value is more than half of the total, so a small change in long-term growth swings everything.

SOFIA: Still, the D-C-F must be the most accurate method. It's based on the company's own cash flows, not the market's mood.

MARCO: Fourth trap. It's the most fundamental method, not the most accurate. Comps and precedents tell you what the market pays. A D-C-F tells you what the company is worth if your assumptions are right. That's what the Supreme Court said in Dell: after a proper sale process, the market price deserves heavy weight. Bankers use the D-C-F as one bar on the football field, alongside the others, never alone.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: walk me through a D-C-F.

SOFIA: You forecast the cash flows, discount them back to today and add them up.

MARCO: Right idea, too short. Make it complete. Project the company's unlevered free cash flow for five to ten years. Calculate a terminal value, with either a perpetuity growth rate or an exit multiple. Discount the cash flows and the terminal value back to today at the weighted average cost of capital. The sum is enterprise value. Subtract net debt and other claims to get equity value, and divide by the diluted share count for a value per share.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Why is a euro today worth more than a euro in a year?

SOFIA: Because of inflation.

MARCO: Give all three reasons. Opportunity cost, because you can invest today's euro and earn a return. Inflation, because money loses purchasing power. And risk, because a future payment is never certain. The discount rate is how you put a price on all three.

SOFIA: Third?

MARCO: If the discount rate goes up by one percentage point, what happens to the D-C-F value?

SOFIA: It goes down. By about one percent?

MARCO: Down, yes, but usually by much more than one percent, because the change compounds over every year and hits the terminal value hardest. And the effect is biggest for companies whose cash flows are far in the future, like young growth companies. That's why their share prices fell so hard when interest rates jumped in twenty twenty-two. More on that in episode sixteen.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: From Norway. On the second of October, AKVA group, which makes technology for fish farming, on land and at sea, agreed to be bought by Yanmar, the Japanese industrial group best known for its engines. Yanmar is family-owned and was founded in nineteen twelve.

SOFIA: Price?

MARCO: One hundred and sixty-one Norwegian kroner a share, in cash. That values all the shares at about five point nine billion kroner.

SOFIA: And the premium? Against the unaffected price, please.

MARCO: Episode twelve is paying off. Fifty-seven percent above one hundred and two kroner fifty, the closing price on the seventh of April. Why that date?

SOFIA: Because something happened the next day that moved the shares.

MARCO: Exactly. On the eighth of April, AKVA announced a strategic review, which is a polite way of saying it was open to offers. Against the volume-weighted average price over the previous thirty days, the premium is sixty-two percent. Over sixty days, sixty-seven percent.

SOFIA: Will it go through?

MARCO: Shareholders holding about ninety-two percent of the shares, including Egersund Group, have already committed to accept. Yanmar needs at least ninety percent, plus competition and foreign investment approvals, and expects to complete in the fourth quarter of this year.

SOFIA: And the D-C-F angle?

MARCO: Here's your interview question. A fifty-seven percent premium means Yanmar is paying far more than the market thought AKVA was worth. How can that make sense?

SOFIA: Because Yanmar's own D-C-F says AKVA is worth more. Maybe it expects faster growth.

MARCO: Good, and be specific. Yanmar's D-C-F can include the synergies it expects, from combining complementary technologies and its global reach with AKVA's business. Those extra cash flows exist only for Yanmar, so AKVA is worth more to it than to an investor on the stock market. Whether Yanmar's assumptions are right is another question. That's the Dell lesson again.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: you invest one hundred euros at ten percent a year for two years. What do you have at the end? Question two: what's the present value of one hundred and ten euros received in one year, at a discount rate of ten percent? Question three: the discount rate goes up. Does the D-C-F value go up or down? Question four: Dell was taken private at thirteen dollars seventy-five a share, and the judge said it was worth seventeen dollars sixty-two. Roughly what percentage above the deal price is that? Question five: you discount unlevered free cash flow at the weighted average cost of capital. Do you get enterprise value or equity value?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: one hundred and twenty-one euros, not one hundred and twenty, because in the second year you also earn interest on the first year's interest. Two: one hundred euros. Divide by one point one; don't subtract ten percent. Three: down. You divide by a bigger number, so the future cash is worth less today. Four: about twenty-eight percent. The gap is three dollars eighty-seven, divided by thirteen seventy-five. Five: enterprise value. Then bridge to equity value by subtracting net debt.

SOFIA: So a euro today beats a euro tomorrow. And a judge's D-C-F beats an expert's.

MARCO: Only until the Supreme Court weighs in. Tomorrow, episode fourteen: our week two review. Equity value and enterprise value, the bridge, multiples, comps, precedents and today's D-C-F, all in one go, with the questions that catch people out.
