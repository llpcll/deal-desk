# The Deal Desk, episode 41: Numerical tests, or how R-B-S bid for A-B-N Amro on two folders and a C-D
In two thousand and seven R-B-S led a seventy-one billion euro takeover on two lever arch folders and a C-D. Then Eurocommercial buys a Belgian shopping centre, and we read the deal like a test question.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode forty-one. Yesterday, the sums you do in your head. Today, the same sums against a clock, on a screen, often before anyone at the bank has met you. Online numerical tests. We start with a bid that had very little data to read.

SOFIA: Which one?

MARCO: Two thousand and seven. The Royal Bank of Scotland, R-B-S, led a group with Santander and Fortis in a seventy-one billion euro takeover of A-B-N Amro, the Dutch bank. It was a contested bid, and a contested bidder doesn't get the run of the target's books. When the British regulator, the F-S-A, later reported on the failure of R-B-S, it said the information A-B-N Amro made available that April amounted to two lever arch folders and a C-D.

SOFIA: For seventy-one billion euros? Surely they asked for more.

MARCO: Johnny Cameron, who ran investment banking at R-B-S, said they weren't able to do due diligence, and that this was part of doing a hostile acquisition. The F-S-A said the decision could reasonably be criticised as a gamble. Then the credit crisis arrived, and the government had to bail the bank out in two thousand and eight. In February two thousand and nine, R-B-S reported a loss of twenty-four point one billion pounds for the year, described at the time as the biggest in British corporate history. Sixteen point two billion of it was write-downs, mainly from the A-B-N Amro deal.

SOFIA: And what does that have to do with a test?

MARCO: A numerical test is the same problem in miniature. A small pile of data, a clock and a decision. The question R-B-S had to answer was: what does this data tell me, and what doesn't it? Every test question asks that, and tests whether you read what's in front of you before you act.

SOFIA: So what will I actually sit?

MARCO: An online test, usually set soon after you apply. Your invitation names the provider and the rules, and the name you'll hear most is S-H-L. Its interactive numerical reasoning test is listed at eighteen minutes, for at most ten questions. It's adaptive, so the difficulty moves with your answers, and instead of picking from four options, you often build the answer by adjusting a chart on screen. Prep sites also describe multiple-choice versions, with around twenty to twenty-five questions in twenty to thirty-five minutes.
SOFIA: Eighteen minutes for ten questions. A hundred and eight seconds each. That sounds like plenty.

MARCO: Right sum, and we'll see about plenty. Here's a question in round numbers. A group has three divisions, with revenue in millions of euros. Rail: four hundred in twenty twenty-four, five hundred in twenty twenty-five. Coach: three hundred, then three hundred and sixty. Bus: one hundred, then one hundred and forty. Which division grew fastest?

SOFIA: Rail. Up one hundred million, the biggest jump.

MARCO: First trap. You answered how much, and the question asked how fast. Fast means a percentage, and you know the follow-up from yesterday: percent of what? Rail, one hundred on four hundred, twenty-five percent. Coach, sixty on three hundred, twenty percent. Bus, forty on one hundred, forty percent. The smallest line in the table grew fastest. And notice the order. Read the question first, then find the two cells you need. Don't study the table and then read.

SOFIA: Same table. What happened to Rail's share of group revenue? It went up by twenty-five percent, because its revenue did.

MARCO: Second trap. Add up the columns, because the table often won't do it for you. The group was eight hundred, and it's now one thousand. Rail was four hundred of eight hundred, a half. It's now five hundred of one thousand, also a half. Its revenue grew at the same pace as the group, so its share didn't move. A share has two numbers in it, and you checked one.

SOFIA: What else do they try?

MARCO: Currencies. A company earns five hundred million dollars, and the rate is one point two five dollars to the euro. What's that in euros?

SOFIA: Five hundred times one point two five. Six hundred and twenty-five million euros.

MARCO: Third trap: direction. One euro buys more than a dollar, so you must end up with fewer euros than dollars. Divide. Dividing by one point two five is the same as taking eighty percent, so four hundred million euros. Say the direction to yourself before you touch the calculator. The same goes for units: check whether the table is in thousands or millions, and whether the price is in pence or pounds.

SOFIA: And if I get a hard one, I stay on it until I have it. Every question counts.

MARCO: Fourth trap. A hundred and eight seconds is a budget, and it has to cover reading the question as well as the sum. If you're stuck when it runs out, pick your best estimate and move on. Minutes spent on one question are minutes you can't spend on the rest, and prep guides warn that on an adaptive test, getting stuck can keep you from the harder questions. And if you've built a chart, read the values on screen before you submit. A dragged handle can land near the number and not on it.

SOFIA: How is it marked? What percentage do I need?

MARCO: It isn't usually a percentage. Prep guides say your result comes back as a percentile, against a comparison group the employer chooses. One prep site puts banks at the eighty-fifth percentile or above. Treat that as a guide, not a rule.

SOFIA: Then how do I prepare?

MARCO: Find your exact format and practise that one, on a screen and against a clock. If your instructions allow a calculator, use the one you'll have on the day. And keep a log of every mistake by type: base, share, direction, units.
MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: a company's revenue goes from eighty to one hundred, and its costs from sixty to seventy. What happened to the margin?

SOFIA: Revenue is up twenty-five percent, costs up about seventeen. So the margin is up about eight percent.

MARCO: Those are growth rates, and you can't subtract them to get a margin. Strong answer: profit was twenty on eighty, so twenty-five percent. Now it's thirty on one hundred, so thirty percent. The margin is up five percentage points. Then one sentence on why: costs grew more slowly than revenue, so more of each extra euro reached profit.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Why would a bank set a test like this, when everyone has Excel?

SOFIA: To check I can do arithmetic.

MARCO: Partly, but the arithmetic is easy. Strong answer: it tests reading. Can you take an unfamiliar table, pick the right two numbers, get the base and the units right, and do it under a clock? That's much of a first-year analyst's work, and a wrong figure in a pitch book ends up in front of a client. Then bring it back to you: I keep a log of my own errors, and here's the one I've fixed.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: One you can read like a test question. On Thursday the twenty-ninth of October, after the market closed, Eurocommercial Properties, a shopping centre investor listed in Amsterdam, Brussels and Milan, agreed to buy Les Grands Prés, a shopping centre in Mons, in Belgium, from Union Investment. More than one hundred shops, about twenty-seven thousand square metres, ninety-seven percent occupied by area. The price is two hundred and sixty-five million euros, including repayment of a shareholder loan.

SOFIA: How does it pay?

MARCO: Partly with new shares. Five point two million of them, sold to institutional investors at twenty-four euros seventy, a five percent discount to Thursday's close of twenty-six euros. So, test question: how much did that raise?

SOFIA: Five point two million times twenty-six. About one hundred and thirty-five million.

MARCO: Fifth trap: wrong cell. Twenty-six is the close, and the new investors paid twenty-four seventy. Round first: five times twenty-five is one hundred and twenty-five, and a little more gets you to about one hundred and twenty-eight million euros. Next question: what share of the price is that?

SOFIA: One hundred and twenty-eight on two hundred and sixty-five. A bit under half.

MARCO: Good. The other one hundred and thirty-seven million or so comes from existing resources and credit lines. That's why net loan to value, debt against the value of the property, barely moves: from forty-one point five percent to about forty-two point one.

SOFIA: So up zero point six percent.

MARCO: Zero point six of a percentage point. Now the return. Eurocommercial gives an initial yield of seven point five percent. Roughly how much income is that?

SOFIA: Ten percent of two hundred and sixty-five is twenty-six and a half. So five percent is about thirteen, and two and a half is about six and a half. About twenty million euros a year.

MARCO: Route, units and a round answer. Turn the yield upside down and you have a multiple: one over seven point five percent is about thirteen times income. Now check it from another table. The centre's sales are eight thousand euros a square metre, and its occupancy cost ratio, what tenants pay as a share of their sales, is ten and a half percent. Eight thousand times twenty-seven thousand square metres is about two hundred and sixteen million euros of sales, if you apply it to the whole area. Ten and a half percent of that is about twenty-three million.

SOFIA: Twenty-three, against twenty.

MARCO: Close enough for a rough check, and that ratio usually covers service charges as well as rent. These are my sums, not the company's, but two routes landing near each other is what you're looking for.

SOFIA: And why does Eurocommercial want it?

MARCO: Its chief executive, Evert Jan van Garderen, says the centre fits the company's D-N-A. Belgium becomes its third-largest market by value, behind Italy and Sweden, and the plan is to raise that yield by reworking the mix of tenants. The company says the deal adds to earnings from day one, with completion expected by the fourth of November.
MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: Rail's revenue rises from four hundred to five hundred, and Bus's from one hundred to one hundred and forty. Which grew faster? Question two: Rail was four hundred of a group's eight hundred, and it's now five hundred of one thousand. What happened to its share? Question three: what's five hundred million dollars in euros, at one point two five dollars to the euro? Question four: you've used up your time on a question and you're still stuck. What do you do? Question five: Eurocommercial sold five point two million new shares at twenty-four euros seventy. Roughly how much did that raise?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: Bus, up forty percent against twenty-five. Rail's one hundred million is the bigger jump, not the faster growth. Two: nothing. It stayed at a half, because Rail grew at the same pace as the group. Three: four hundred million euros. One euro buys more than a dollar, so you divide. Four: pick your best estimate and move on. Time spent stuck comes out of the later questions. Five: about one hundred and twenty-eight million euros. The twenty-six euro close isn't what the new investors paid.
