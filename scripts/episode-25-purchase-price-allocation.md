# The Deal Desk, episode 25: Purchase price allocation and goodwill, or the day Kraft Heinz wrote down its brands
Kraft Heinz booked thirty billion dollars of goodwill on Kraft, then wrote fifteen billion off its goodwill and brands. Then Merck KGaA pays one point three billion dollars for a biotech with one promising drug.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-five. Yesterday we said a deal creates new accounting charges. Today, where they come from: purchase price allocation, and goodwill. And we start with a write-down that took more than a quarter off a company's share price in a day.

SOFIA: Kraft Heinz.

MARCO: Kraft Heinz. Heinz was owned by Berkshire Hathaway and three G Capital, and in twenty fifteen it merged with Kraft. The deal closed on the second of July twenty fifteen, and Berkshire and three G ended up with about fifty-one percent. In the accounts, Heinz was the buyer, and the total price for Kraft came to about fifty-two point six billion dollars.

SOFIA: So Kraft was worth fifty-two point six billion.

MARCO: That's the question the accounting has to answer. Kraft Heinz valued everything Kraft owned and owed at fair value on the day of the deal. Net assets came to about twenty-two point two billion. The rest, about thirty point five billion, was booked as goodwill, mainly for expected synergies and growth, the filing said.

SOFIA: Twenty-two billion of net assets. Factories, mostly?

MARCO: Mostly not. Factories and equipment, about four point two billion. Identifiable intangible assets, about forty-seven point eight billion, and forty-three point one billion of that was trademarks with indefinite lives. Against those, about nine point three billion of debt and sixteen point seven billion of deferred tax liabilities. Keep that last line in mind.

SOFIA: And then?

MARCO: On the twenty-first of February twenty nineteen, Kraft Heinz reported its results for twenty eighteen, with non-cash impairment charges of fifteen point four billion dollars. They hit goodwill, mainly in its American refrigerated and Canadian retail businesses, and the Kraft and Oscar Mayer trademarks. A net loss for the quarter of twelve point six billion. The same day it cut its dividend by thirty-six percent and disclosed a subpoena from the Securities and Exchange Commission about its procurement accounting. The next day the shares fell about twenty-seven and a half percent.

SOFIA: And Warren Buffett?

MARCO: The following Monday, on television: "We overpaid for Kraft." So, how does a price become goodwill, and how does goodwill become a loss? Round numbers, millions of euros. Atlas, our buyer from yesterday, buys all of Cedar for one thousand. Cedar's own balance sheet says its net assets, assets minus liabilities, are three hundred. That's book value.

SOFIA: So goodwill is seven hundred. Price minus book value.

MARCO: First trap. Before you get to goodwill, you revalue everything at fair value on the day of the deal. That's the purchase price allocation. Cedar's factories sit on its books at old cost less years of depreciation. Say they're worth fifty more today. And the big one: things Cedar built itself never appear on its own balance sheet. Its brand, its customer contracts, its technology. A buyer that pays for them has to recognise them. Say the brand is worth two hundred and fifty, and the customer relationships one hundred.

SOFIA: Fifty, plus two hundred and fifty, plus one hundred. Four hundred of write-ups. Net assets of seven hundred, so goodwill is three hundred.

MARCO: Second trap: tax. The accounts will now charge depreciation and amortisation on the new, higher values, but the tax authority still only allows deductions on the old ones. To square the two, you book a deferred tax liability: the write-ups times the tax rate. At twenty-five percent, one hundred. So fair value of net assets is three hundred, plus four hundred, minus one hundred: six hundred. Goodwill is one thousand minus six hundred. Four hundred. Kraft Heinz's sixteen point seven billion of deferred tax sits on the same line, on a much bigger scale. And after the deal?

SOFIA: Goodwill gets amortised, like the factories get depreciated.

MARCO: Third trap. Under international rules, and American rules for listed companies, goodwill isn't amortised. It's tested for impairment at least once a year. Assets with a limited life are amortised: the customer relationships, say over ten years, ten a year before tax. That's yesterday's new amortisation. But a brand the buyer expects to use with no end in sight is treated like goodwill: no amortisation, an annual test. Kraft's forty-three billion of trademarks were exactly that.

SOFIA: So moving value between goodwill and customer relationships changes earnings.

MARCO: Exactly. Move another hundred from goodwill to customer relationships and Atlas's pre-tax profit falls by another ten a year, for ten years. The allocation decides how much amortisation hits earnings per share for a decade. Now the test. A year later, Cedar's market turns. Atlas compares what Cedar is carried at in its accounts, net assets plus goodwill, one thousand, with what the business is worth now. Say eight hundred.

SOFIA: Then Atlas has lost two hundred of cash.

MARCO: Fourth trap. The cash left a year ago, when Atlas paid. The impairment writes goodwill down by two hundred, from four hundred to two hundred. It's an expense on the income statement, so profit falls. But no money moves. It tells you the price was too high for what the business now earns. An impairment is a confession that arrives late.

SOFIA: So Kraft Heinz didn't lose fifteen billion of cash that day.

MARCO: No. The money was spent in twenty fifteen. The shares fell because of what the charge said: the brands would earn less than the price assumed. And it came with a dividend cut and a subpoena.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what is goodwill, and how is it created?

SOFIA: It's the value of a company's reputation and its brand.

MARCO: The brand is exactly what's not in goodwill, if you can identify it. Strong answer: goodwill is the part of the purchase price left over after you allocate it to every identifiable asset and liability at fair value, including brands and customer relationships the target never recorded, and after the deferred tax on the write-ups. It stands for synergies, the workforce, growth, and any overpayment. It only arises in an acquisition. Listed companies don't amortise it; they test it for impairment at least once a year.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Goodwill is impaired by one hundred. Walk me through the three statements.

SOFIA: Net income falls seventy-five after tax. And cash falls by one hundred, because the asset is gone.

MARCO: Two slips. Strong answer: on the income statement, a one hundred expense. Goodwill impairment is usually not deductible for tax, so net income falls by the full one hundred. On the cash flow statement, start from net income, down one hundred, and add the impairment back because it's non-cash: cash is unchanged. On the balance sheet, goodwill is down one hundred and retained earnings are down one hundred, so it balances. And if the interviewer tells you it is deductible, net income falls seventy-five and cash goes up twenty-five, through lower tax.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A German buyer and a British target. On Monday, the nineteenth of October, Merck KGaA, the science and technology group based in Darmstadt, not the American Merck, agreed to buy Silence Therapeutics, a London biotech whose shares trade in New York. Twenty dollars in cash for each New York share, an equity value of about one point three billion dollars and an enterprise value of one point one billion. That's a premium of one hundred and thirty percent to the closing price on Friday the sixteenth of October, and eighty-two percent to the thirty-day volume-weighted average of ten dollars ninety-nine.

SOFIA: One hundred and thirty percent. And the enterprise value is lower than the equity value, so Silence must have two hundred million of debt.

MARCO: Other way round. Enterprise value is equity value plus debt minus cash. When it's lower, the company has net cash, here about two hundred million. Silence had seventy-two million of cash at the end of June, and in August it raised about one hundred and seventy-five million before costs, selling new shares at thirteen dollars fifty. Merck's price is about forty-eight percent above that.

SOFIA: And what is Merck buying?

MARCO: Mainly one drug, divesiran, for polycythemia vera, a rare blood cancer where the body makes too many red blood cells. In a Phase Two trial reported in August, eighty-eight percent of patients responded, against nineteen percent on placebo. Merck says the drug is ready for Phase Three, and calls the deal an entry point into hematology. Closing is expected in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven, after a shareholder vote, the approval of the High Court in London, and regulatory approvals.

SOFIA: So for the allocation, it's easy. No sales, nothing else to put it in. It's all goodwill.

MARCO: That's your interview question, and the trap from the start of the episode. Strong answer: first, the net cash, about two hundred million, at face value. Then the drug. An acquired drug in development is an identifiable intangible asset, usually called in-process research and development. Under the international rules Merck uses, it isn't amortised until it's ready for use, but it's tested for impairment every year. Then deferred tax on that write-up, and goodwill for what's left: the scientists and the technology behind the next drugs. One twist. When almost all the value sits in a single asset, the rules let the buyer treat the deal as buying an asset rather than a business, and then no goodwill appears at all. Merck hasn't published an allocation, so look for it in its accounts after closing.

SOFIA: And if Phase Three fails?

MARCO: Then that intangible gets written down. Kraft Heinz, in biotech form. Quiz. For you listening.
