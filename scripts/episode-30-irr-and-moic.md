# The Deal Desk, episode 30: The internal rate of return and the money multiple, or why Hilton's three times took eleven years
Blackstone made about fourteen billion dollars on Hilton, described as the most profitable buyout ever, and it took eleven years. Then the owner of National Express agrees a firm forty-six pence offer from a Spanish family and an infrastructure fund.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode thirty. Yesterday Harbour's fund made two and a half times its money. Today, the next question: over how long? The money multiple and the internal rate of return. And we start with what was called the most profitable buyout ever.

SOFIA: Which one?

MARCO: Hilton. On the third of July twenty oh seven, Hilton Hotels agreed to an all-cash buyout by Blackstone. Forty-seven dollars and fifty cents a share, thirty-two percent above that day's close. About twenty-six billion dollars, including debt.

SOFIA: July twenty oh seven. Just before the financial crisis.

MARCO: Just before. Travel fell, and a hotel company with that much debt was in trouble. In April twenty ten the debt was restructured. Blackstone put in eight hundred million dollars of new equity, and the debt was cut to sixteen billion.

SOFIA: Yesterday's trap. Leverage works in reverse.

MARCO: It came close. But Blackstone held on. Hilton went public again on the twelfth of December twenty thirteen, raising about two point three five billion dollars, and Blackstone sold out in stages from twenty fourteen. A quarter of the company went to H-N-A Group in twenty sixteen for six point five billion dollars, and the last block in May twenty eighteen, for about one point three billion.

SOFIA: And the result?

MARCO: About fourteen billion dollars of profit over eleven years. Blackstone more than tripled its money.

SOFIA: Three times. Better than Harbour's two and a half.

MARCO: Is it? Harbour took five years. Hilton took eleven. A fund's return is measured with two numbers. The multiple of money, M-O-I-C, says how much came back for every pound put in. The internal rate of return, I-R-R, says how fast it grew.

SOFIA: So how does the I-R-R work?

MARCO: Back to Harbour, in millions of pounds. The fund puts in four hundred and five years later takes out one thousand. The multiple?

SOFIA: One thousand over four hundred. Two and a half times.

MARCO: Now the I-R-R.

SOFIA: A gain of one hundred and fifty percent over five years. Thirty percent a year.

MARCO: First trap. That's simple interest, and money compounds. Episode thirteen: four hundred growing at thirty percent a year for five years is nearly one thousand five hundred, not one thousand. The I-R-R is the yearly rate that turns four hundred into one thousand in five years, with compounding. Here, about twenty percent. Check it: four hundred, then four hundred and eighty, five hundred and seventy-six, about six hundred and ninety, eight hundred and thirty, and nine hundred and ninety-five.

SOFIA: So it's just a growth rate.

MARCO: With one cheque in and one cheque out, yes. The full definition: the discount rate that makes the net present value of all the fund's cash flows, in and out, equal to zero. That matters when money moves more than twice, and we'll get there.

MARCO: First, yesterday's promise. Same two and a half times, different holds. Over three years?

SOFIA: Higher than twenty.

MARCO: About thirty-six percent a year. Over five years, about twenty. Over seven, about fourteen. Same multiple, same six hundred of profit, and the I-R-R falls by more than half.

SOFIA: And Hilton?

MARCO: Suppose all the money had come back in one go, in year eleven, at three times. That's about ten and a half percent a year. In fact it came back in stages, from twenty fourteen on, so the real figure is higher. But nowhere near three times in five years, which is about twenty-five percent.

SOFIA: So the I-R-R is the number that counts.

MARCO: Second trap. Both count, for different reasons. Take a fund that turns one hundred into one hundred and fifty in a single year. An I-R-R of fifty percent. Brilliant?

SOFIA: Yes.

MARCO: It made fifty. Harbour made six hundred. And after a year the investors hold cash again, which has to find another deal as good. The I-R-R says how fast the money grew while it was at work. The multiple says how much money you actually made. A short hold flatters the I-R-R; a long hold flatters the multiple. Investors look at both.

MARCO: Now timing inside the hold. Two years in, Harbour borrows two hundred more and pays it to the fund as a dividend. A dividend recapitalisation. Ignore the extra interest to keep it simple. At the exit there's two hundred more debt, so the fund gets eight hundred, not one thousand. The multiple?

SOFIA: The dividend is extra money. Two hundred plus one thousand, so three times.

MARCO: Third trap. The new debt comes off the exit price. Two hundred in year two, eight hundred in year five. One thousand in total, still two and a half times. But the I-R-R rises from about twenty percent to just over twenty-four, because two hundred came back three years early. Early cash lifts the I-R-R and leaves the multiple where it was.

SOFIA: And that's where the net present value definition comes in.

MARCO: Exactly. Three cash flows, so you need the rate at which they net to zero. A spreadsheet finds it. In an interview, use shortcuts. Double your money in three years: about twenty-six percent. In five: about fifteen. Triple in three years: about forty-four. Triple in five: about twenty-five. And the rule of seventy-two: seventy-two divided by the rate gives the years it takes to double.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what's the difference between the I-R-R and the money multiple, and which would you rather have?

SOFIA: The I-R-R, because it takes time into account and the multiple doesn't.

MARCO: True, and half an answer. Strong answer: the multiple is total cash returned over cash invested, so it measures how much, and ignores time. The I-R-R is the annual return, the rate that sets the net present value of the cash flows to zero, so it rewards speed. Each misleads on its own. A quick flip can show a huge I-R-R on little profit, and a long hold a big multiple with a modest I-R-R, like Hilton. I'd want a strong I-R-R on a meaningful multiple, and I'd check how much of it came from early cash rather than a better business.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Mental maths, and expect it in private equity interviews. A fund doubles its money in five years. What's the I-R-R?

SOFIA: One hundred percent over five years. Twenty percent.

MARCO: The first trap again. Twenty percent for five years compounds to about two and a half times. Strong answer: about fifteen percent. Show the working: seventy-two over five years is just over fourteen, so a little above that. Say the method out loud. The interviewer cares more about it than the decimal.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A follow-up. Two days ago, in episode twenty-eight, Mobico, the owner of National Express, had a non-binding proposal of forty-six pence a share. On Friday the thirtieth of October it became a firm offer. Leita Mobility, a subsidiary of Autobuses de León, will pay forty-six pence a share in cash, and Mobico's independent directors unanimously recommend it.

SOFIA: The Cosmen family and Asterion.

MARCO: Autobuses de León is fifty point one percent owned by C-M-C, the Cosmen family's investment group, which already holds about twenty-six percent of Mobico. The other forty-nine point nine percent belongs to Asterion's third infrastructure fund.

SOFIA: And the price? You said two hundred and eighty-two million.

MARCO: Good catch. That was on about six hundred and fourteen million shares in issue. The firm offer counts them fully diluted, including shares for employee share plans: about six hundred and seventy million. So about three hundred and eight million pounds. Same price, more shares.

SOFIA: And the premium is about fifty-five percent.

MARCO: Fifty-five point one, over twenty-nine point seven pence on the twentieth of October, the last business day before the offer period. But check earlier. The first proposal came on the fourth of August, at thirty-eight pence. Against the close of twenty-three point eight pence the day before, forty-six pence is a premium of ninety-three point three percent.

MARCO: Now the number that matters today. The announcement puts the enterprise value at about two point three billion pounds. On top of the equity sit adjusted net debt of one point one three four billion, a hybrid bond of five hundred million and provisions of two hundred and eighty-five million, among other items. That's about eleven point seven times adjusted EBIT of one hundred and ninety-eight million.

SOFIA: So the equity is less than a seventh of the value.

MARCO: And here's your interview question. Suppose the business becomes worth ten percent more, and the claims ahead of the equity stay the same. What happens to the equity?

SOFIA: It's worth ten percent more.

MARCO: Ten percent of two point three billion is about two hundred and thirty million, and all of it goes to the shareholders. On three hundred and eight million of equity, that's a rise of about seventy-five percent. My arithmetic, not the announcement's. And a ten percent fall would take about three quarters of the equity. A small move in the business, a big move in the multiple and the I-R-R.

SOFIA: How is it paid for?

MARCO: From Autobuses de León's existing cash and an interim facilities agreement, like Ashtead yesterday. After completion, the plan is to replace Mobico's borrowings with long-term, infrastructure-style debt, probably a bank term loan and U-S private placement notes.

SOFIA: Can C-M-C vote its twenty-six percent?

MARCO: Not at the court meeting. It's on the buyer's side, so the other shareholders decide: a majority in number, holding seventy-five percent in value of the shares voted. Then the court and the regulators. Completion is expected in the first half of twenty twenty-seven. On Friday morning the shares rose about eleven percent.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: a fund puts in four hundred and takes out one thousand five years later. Roughly what is the I-R-R? Question two: a fund doubles its money in five years. Roughly what is the I-R-R? Question three: Harbour pays the fund a debt-funded dividend in year two, and the total cash back is unchanged. What happens to the multiple and the I-R-R? Question four: Blackstone more than tripled its money on Hilton. Why is its I-R-R well below what three times in five years would give? Question five: Mobico's equity is worth about three hundred and eight million pounds, its enterprise value about two point three billion. If the enterprise value rises ten percent and the debt doesn't change, roughly how much does the equity rise?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: about twenty percent. Thirty percent a year forgets compounding. Two: about fifteen percent, not twenty. Three: the multiple stays at two and a half times, and the I-R-R rises to just over twenty-four percent, because cash came back earlier. Four: time. The money was at work for about eleven years. Five: about seventy-five percent. Two hundred and thirty million of extra value, all of it for the equity.

SOFIA: So the multiple is how much, the I-R-R is how fast, and debt makes both swing.
