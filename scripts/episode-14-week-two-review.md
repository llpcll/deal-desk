# The Deal Desk, episode 14: Week two review, one café chain and every valuation tool
Equity value, the bridge, multiples, comps, precedents and the D-C-F, all on one company. Then Schneider Electric's twenty-three billion dollar bet on software.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode fourteen. Review day. Week two was all about value: equity value and enterprise value, the bridge, multiples, comps, precedents and, yesterday, the D-C-F. Today I put Sofia through all of it, the way an interviewer would. If you're listening, answer in your head before she does.

SOFIA: Same rules as last week, then.

MARCO: Same rules. And we start where the week started. January twenty twenty-two. Microsoft agrees to buy Activision Blizzard for ninety-five dollars a share in cash. The press release says sixty-eight point seven billion dollars, inclusive of Activision Blizzard's net cash. Equity value or enterprise value?

SOFIA: Closer to enterprise value. Ninety-five dollars times all the shares came to more than seventy billion. That's the equity value, what the shareholders received. Activision had more cash than debt, so taking the net cash off gives the smaller number.

MARCO: Good. And the lesson?

SOFIA: When a headline gives you a price, ask which value it is.

MARCO: Keep that question ready, because today's deal of the day comes with two headline numbers as well. Round one. One company, every tool from the week. Cafe Group. Share price ten euros, one hundred shares. Equity value?

SOFIA: One thousand.

MARCO: Debt four hundred, cash one hundred. It owns eighty percent of a bakery business, and outside investors own the rest, a stake worth fifty. It also holds a small stake in a coffee roaster, worth fifty. Enterprise value?

SOFIA: One thousand plus four hundred, minus one hundred. The bakery stake isn't ours, so minus fifty. And minus fifty for the roaster. One thousand two hundred.

MARCO: First trap, and it caught you in episode nine as well. The outside stake in the bakery, the non-controlling interest, gets added. Cafe Group consolidates all of the bakery's EBITDA, including the part it doesn't own, so enterprise value has to include that part too. One thousand, plus four hundred, plus fifty, minus one hundred, minus fifty. One thousand three hundred.

SOFIA: And the roaster comes off because its EBITDA isn't in our numbers, but its value is in our share price.

MARCO: Exactly. One rule covers the whole bridge: whatever is in the EBITDA has to be in the enterprise value. Now Cafe Group issues two hundred of new shares and keeps the cash. What happens to enterprise value?

SOFIA: It goes up by two hundred. The company has more money.

MARCO: Second trap. Equity value goes up two hundred, cash goes up two hundred, and enterprise value doesn't move. The cafés are the same, the customers are the same. Enterprise value changes when the business changes, not when the financing does.

SOFIA: Episode eight. I knew that one.

MARCO: Everyone knows it until they're nervous. Round two, multiples. Cafe Group's EBITDA is one hundred and thirty. E-V over EBITDA?

SOFIA: One thousand three hundred over one hundred and thirty. Ten times.

MARCO: Could you divide enterprise value by net income instead?

SOFIA: No. Net income is after interest, so it belongs only to shareholders. Enterprise value goes with numbers before interest, like EBITDA. Equity value goes with net income, which is the P-E ratio.

MARCO: Clean. Round three, comps. Five listed café chains trade at eight, nine, ten, eleven and twenty times EBITDA.

SOFIA: Eight plus nine plus ten plus eleven plus twenty is fifty-eight. Divided by five, eleven point six times.

MARCO: Third trap. That's the average, and the twenty is dragging it up. Bankers use the median, which here is ten. So ten times one hundred and thirty, one thousand three hundred of enterprise value. Bridge back: take off the debt and the outside stake in the bakery, add back the cash and the roaster. Three hundred of net claims, so one thousand of equity value.

SOFIA: Ten euros a share. Exactly where Cafe Group trades today.

MARCO: Which makes sense, because comps are built from share prices: what investors pay for small stakes. No control premium. Round four, precedents. Past takeovers of café chains have a median of twelve times EBITDA.

SOFIA: Twelve times one hundred and thirty is one thousand five hundred and sixty. Minus three hundred, one thousand two hundred and sixty of equity value. Twelve euros sixty a share.

MARCO: And the premium over today's price?

SOFIA: Two euros sixty over ten. Twenty-six percent. And if the shares had already jumped on bid rumours, I'd measure it against the unaffected price, from before the rumours.

MARCO: That's the Spire lesson, and you said it without being asked. Why are precedents higher than comps?

SOFIA: The control premium. And the synergies a buyer expects, part of which it pays away to win.

MARCO: Round five, the D-C-F. A hundred euros arriving in two years, discount rate ten percent. Present value?

SOFIA: Divide by one point one twice, so by one point two one. About eighty-three.

MARCO: Good. Now put the methods together. Comps give roughly nine to eleven euros a share. Precedents, roughly eleven to fourteen. Say our D-C-F, across a sensible range of assumptions, gives ten to fifteen. Which one do you trust most?

SOFIA: The D-C-F. It's based on the company's own cash, not on what other people paid.

MARCO: Fourth trap. It's the most fundamental method, not the most reliable. Remember Dell: two experts, the same method, twenty-eight dollars and twelve dollars. A D-C-F is only as good as its assumptions. You put every method on the football field, one bar each, and see where the offer sits. An offer of twelve euros sixty is above comps and inside the precedent and D-C-F ranges. That's the picture a board wants before it recommends a deal.

SOFIA: So my mistakes this week were all about which side things go on.

MARCO: Matching and direction. Adding or subtracting on the bridge. Pairing the right value with the right profit. Average or median. Dividing, not subtracting. Fix those and the valuation part of an interview gets much calmer.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: how would you value a company?

SOFIA: Comps, precedents and a D-C-F, and take the average of the three.

MARCO: Right methods, wrong ending. Don't average them. Say you'd use trading comparables for what the market pays for similar companies today, precedent transactions for what buyers have paid for control, and a D-C-F for what the business is worth on its own cash flows. Add the share price range for context. Show each as a range on a football field, explain why they differ, and weight them by the situation: precedents matter most when you're advising on a sale.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Which method usually gives the highest value?

SOFIA: Precedents. Every time.

MARCO: Usually, not every time. Precedents tend to be highest because they include a control premium and synergies. But a D-C-F can come out higher for a fast-growing company with optimistic assumptions. And if market multiples have risen since the precedent deals were done, comps can beat precedents. The interviewer wants the reason, not the ranking.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: Announced yesterday. Schneider Electric, the French energy management group, the same buyer we saw with Shelly in episode ten, agreed to buy PTC, an American company that makes software for designing, manufacturing and servicing products. Two hundred and five dollars a share, all in cash.

SOFIA: And the two headline numbers?

MARCO: About twenty-two point six billion dollars of equity value, and about twenty-three point seven billion dollars of enterprise value. It's expected to be the largest acquisition in Schneider's history.

SOFIA: So about one point one billion between them. PTC has more debt and other claims than cash.

MARCO: Right. The premium?

SOFIA: Against the unaffected price, please.

MARCO: Forty-two point three percent over PTC's last closing price before the announcement, and forty-six point one percent over the volume-weighted average price of the previous thirty trading days. PTC had revenue of about two point four billion euros in twenty twenty-five. Schneider puts the price at twenty-one times PTC's expected adjusted EBITA for twenty twenty-seven. That's operating profit after depreciation but before amortisation. Including all the cost savings it expects, thirteen times.

SOFIA: What savings?

MARCO: About two hundred and fifty million euros a year by year three. Plus around eight hundred million euros of extra revenue from selling more together. It's paying mostly with borrowed money: about sixteen to seventeen billion euros of new debt, and five to six billion euros of new shares. Closing is expected by the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

SOFIA: And how did the market take it?

MARCO: Schneider's shares fell more than nine percent in morning trading in Paris. So here's your interview question, and use the whole week. Is twenty-one times a fair price?

SOFIA: In episode ten, software traded at about twenty-four and a half times EBITDA. So twenty-one times is cheap.

MARCO: Careful. That's the matching trap again. Different profit measure, different year, different set of companies. The strong answer: compare it with software comps on the same metric and the same period; look at precedent software takeovers; and build a D-C-F on PTC's own cash flows, with Schneider expecting revenue to grow about ten percent a year through twenty twenty-nine. Then the context. Analysts at Jefferies said fears of A-I disruption had pushed software valuations down, letting Schneider buy at a decade-low valuation.

SOFIA: Then why did Schneider's shares fall?

MARCO: Because the thirteen times depends on synergies that haven't happened yet, and the deal brings a lot of new debt and new shares. Investors worry the buyer will pay away more than it gains. That's the winner's curse from episode twelve, priced in on the first morning.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: market cap eight hundred, debt three hundred, cash one hundred, non-controlling interests forty, and a stake in an associate worth twenty. What's the enterprise value? Question two: a company issues two hundred of new shares and keeps the cash. What happens to enterprise value? Question three: five peers trade at six, seven, eight, nine and twenty-five times EBITDA. What multiple do you apply? Question four: the unaffected share price is twenty and the offer is twenty-six. What's the premium? Question five: Schneider is paying about twenty-two point six billion dollars of equity value and twenty-three point seven billion of enterprise value for PTC. What does the gap tell you?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: eight hundred plus three hundred plus forty, minus one hundred, minus twenty. One thousand and twenty. Two: nothing. Equity value and cash both rise by two hundred. Three: the median, eight times, not the average of eleven. Four: thirty percent. Six over twenty. Five: PTC carries about one point one billion dollars more in debt and other claims than in cash.

SOFIA: Two weeks ago I'd have subtracted the forty.

MARCO: And now you add it without thinking. Tomorrow, episode fifteen, we open up the D-C-F. Unlevered free cash flow: the cash a business produces for all its investors, built from EBIT down. And you'll see every line of week one's three statements come back.
