# The Deal Desk, episode 28: Week four review, from the first approach to the first day of trading
Kraft Heinz bid one hundred and forty-three billion dollars for Unilever and walked away two days later. Six episodes of buying and selling companies, tested the way an interviewer would. Then the owner of National Express gets a forty-six pence approach from a group backed by its largest shareholder.

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-eight. Review day. Week four was about buying and selling whole companies: synergies, the deal process, accretion and dilution, goodwill, hostile bids and the I-P-O. Today I take Sofia through all of it, no notes. If you're listening, answer before she does.

SOFIA: No notes again. Fine.

MARCO: We start with one story that touches almost every episode. On Friday the seventeenth of February twenty seventeen, Unilever told the market it had received a proposal from Kraft Heinz. Fifty dollars a share: thirty dollars and twenty-three cents in cash, plus zero point two two two of a share in the combined company. An equity value of about one hundred and forty-three billion dollars, and a premium of eighteen percent to the previous day's close.

SOFIA: Cash and shares, like Mittal's first offer for Arcelor.

MARCO: Same idea. Unilever rejected it that day. It said the proposal fundamentally undervalued Unilever, and saw no merit, financial or strategic, for its shareholders. And because the approach was now public, the British Takeover Code started a clock: Kraft Heinz had until five in the afternoon on the seventeenth of March to make a firm offer or walk away.

SOFIA: A rejected approach, made public. The bear hug from episode twenty-six.

MARCO: It looked like the start of one. Unilever's shares rose about thirteen percent during the day. Then on Sunday the nineteenth, two days later, Kraft Heinz withdrew. In its words, it was best to step away early.

SOFIA: Why give up so fast?

MARCO: Look at who owned Kraft Heinz. Berkshire Hathaway, about twenty-seven percent, and three G Capital, about twenty-four. Berkshire had written in its annual report that it would join only with partners making friendly acquisitions. A fight was off the table. So, which of this week's episodes are in this story?

SOFIA: Hostile bids: a public approach and a board saying no. The deal process: a deadline, and a bidder still at the approach stage. Synergies, because three G was known for cutting costs. And goodwill, because two years later Kraft Heinz wrote down fifteen point four billion dollars, including the Kraft and Oscar Mayer trademarks.

MARCO: Good. And the thirteen percent? The offer was eighteen percent above the old price, and the shares only went up thirteen.

SOFIA: The market thought a deal was likely, but not certain.

MARCO: Right. The gap between the share price and the offer is the market's view of the risk. Keep that, because it comes back in today's deal of the day. Now the week's other stories, one line each. A-B InBev and SABMiller?

SOFIA: A fifty percent premium, paid for one point four billion dollars a year of promised savings.

MARCO: Twitter?

SOFIA: Musk tried to walk away, and the contract made him close.

MARCO: LinkedIn and Arm?

SOFIA: Microsoft told its shareholders the deal would dilute earnings for two years. And Arm raised nothing from its own listing, because SoftBank sold the shares.

MARCO: Different stories, one question: who gets paid, and how much. Round one, synergies. Millions of euros. Cost savings of eighty a year before tax, a twenty-five percent tax rate, a ten percent discount rate, no growth. What are they worth?

SOFIA: Sixty after tax, over ten percent. Six hundred, before the one-off costs and the wait.

MARCO: Clean. Say six hundred is the net figure. The target is worth two thousand on its own, and the buyer offers two thousand four hundred. How much of the synergies has it paid away?

SOFIA: Four hundred of premium out of six hundred. Two thirds. And the walk-away price is two thousand six hundred.

MARCO: Round two, the process. Bidder A offers one thousand two hundred, with a financing condition and a one in five chance the money doesn't arrive. Bidder B offers one thousand, fully financed.

SOFIA: Eighty percent of one thousand two hundred is nine hundred and sixty. B, and that's before counting the damage of a failed sale.

MARCO: Right. And after signing, can the buyer walk away by paying the reverse termination fee?

SOFIA: Only in the cases the contract sets out, like the debt not arriving. Otherwise the seller can ask for specific performance. Ask Musk.

MARCO: Round three, accretion and dilution. Atlas earns one hundred and sixty, has one hundred and sixty million shares, and trades at sixteen euros. Earnings per share, one euro. It buys a company earning twenty, for four hundred. Twenty times. All in shares.

SOFIA: Twenty-five million new shares. One hundred and eighty over one hundred and eighty-five: about ninety-seven cents. Dilutive, because a price to earnings of sixteen is buying one of twenty.

MARCO: Now pay with debt at six percent, tax twenty-five percent.

SOFIA: Interest twenty-four, eighteen after tax. One hundred and sixty-two over one hundred and sixty: about one euro and one cent. Accretive. So debt makes it the better deal.

MARCO: First trap. Same company, same price. Only the financing changed. The deal hasn't got better; the yardstick has. Debt at four and a half percent after tax looks cheap, but it adds risk that earnings per share doesn't see. Accretion tells you about the financing, not whether four hundred is the right price.

SOFIA: And Microsoft was the same point the other way round.

MARCO: Exactly. Round four, goodwill. Atlas pays two thousand for a company with book net assets of five hundred. Write-ups of six hundred, mostly a brand and customer relationships. Tax twenty-five percent.

SOFIA: Deferred tax on the write-ups, one hundred and fifty. Fair value of net assets, five hundred plus six hundred minus one hundred and fifty: nine hundred and fifty. Goodwill, one thousand and fifty.

MARCO: Good. Two years later, goodwill is impaired by three hundred, not tax deductible. Cash?

SOFIA: Net income down three hundred. And cash down three hundred, because the value is gone.

MARCO: Second trap. The value is gone; the cash went two years ago. The impairment is added back on the cash flow statement, so cash doesn't move. Goodwill falls to seven hundred and fifty, retained earnings fall by three hundred, and it balances. Kraft Heinz in miniature.

SOFIA: That one's staying with me this time.

MARCO: Round five, hostile bids. Unaffected price forty euros. A leak pushes the shares to forty-six. The bidder offers fifty. Premium?

SOFIA: Twenty-five percent, over the forty. Against forty-six it's under nine percent, which is the number the target's board would love to quote.

MARCO: And then the shares trade at fifty-two?

SOFIA: The market expects a higher bid. And in Britain the board can't issue new shares to block it without shareholder approval. It argues, or finds a white knight.

MARCO: Round six, the I-P-O. A company sells forty million shares at fifteen euros. Ten million are new, thirty million come from the private equity owner. What does the company raise?

SOFIA: Six hundred million.

MARCO: Third trap. The offer raises six hundred million. The company only gets the new shares: one hundred and fifty. The other four hundred and fifty go to the seller. And if the shares close the first day at eighteen?

SOFIA: Three euros on forty million shares. One hundred and twenty million left on the table.

MARCO: So where did you slip?

SOFIA: Debt making a deal better. An impairment as cash. And who gets the money from an I-P-O.

MARCO: Each time, something that looked like money was counted as money. Ask every time: whose money, and when?

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First, the question that joins the week: your client wants to buy a listed competitor. How do you decide what to offer?

SOFIA: Value the target with a D-C-F and comparables, add a premium of about thirty percent, and check it's accretive.

MARCO: A list of tools, not a decision. Strong answer: start with the target on its own, from the football field: comparables, precedents, a D-C-F. Add the synergies, after tax and one-off costs. Standalone value plus net synergies is the walk-away price. The offer sits well below that, but far enough above the unaffected price to win the board, or the shareholders if the board says no. Then test the financing: earnings per share, leverage, the credit rating. And the process: who else might bid, how certain your money is, and what you'll accept in the contract.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: A deal is accretive from year one, and the buyer's shares fall on the announcement. How?

SOFIA: The market's wrong, or doesn't understand the deal.

MARCO: Start by assuming it's right. Strong answer: accretion only compares the target's earnings with the cost of the money. Investors may think the price is too high, so the premium hands value to the seller. The new debt may make the buyer riskier, or the synergies may look doubtful. L-S-E-G fell about four point seven percent on the day its Octus talks came out, before anything was signed.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A British company, a Spanish bidder, and an old shareholder. On Wednesday, the twenty-first of October, Mobico, the Birmingham-based owner of National Express, which also runs buses in Spain, Portugal and Morocco, confirmed a non-binding proposal of forty-six pence a share in cash. The bidder is Autobuses de León, jointly held by C-M-C Group and a fund managed by Asterion Industrial Partners.

SOFIA: Who is C-M-C?

MARCO: Mobico's largest shareholder, with about twenty-six percent. It has been an investor since it sold Alsa, the Spanish coach operator, to the group in twenty oh five. Forty-six pence on about six hundred and fourteen million shares gives an equity value of about two hundred and eighty-two million pounds.

SOFIA: Two hundred and eighty-two million for National Express. That's cheap.

MARCO: That's the equity. A buyer also takes on Mobico's debt, so the business costs a good deal more than the headline. Episode eight, every week.

SOFIA: And the premium?

MARCO: Reports put it at about fifty-six percent over Tuesday's close. On Wednesday morning the shares rose about a third, to around thirty-nine and a half pence.

SOFIA: So against today's price, the premium is only about sixteen percent.

MARCO: Round five again. Thirty-nine and a half is a price that already contains the bid. The premium is measured against the unaffected price. And Mobico says its statement was prompted by recent movement in its share price, so check the days before Tuesday too, in case the leak started earlier.

SOFIA: And the gap between thirty-nine and a half and forty-six is the market doubting the deal. Like Unilever.

MARCO: Exactly the cold open. The proposal is non-binding. Autobuses de León is doing confirmatory due diligence, and under the Takeover Code it has until five in the afternoon on the eighteenth of November to make a firm offer or walk away. The same kind of clock Kraft Heinz faced.

SOFIA: But this time the board likes it.

MARCO: The independent directors, which means the whole board except Jorge Cosmen, say they're minded to recommend forty-six pence if a firm offer comes on those terms. Friendly, then. But not a deal yet. Here's your interview question. The bidder is backed by a shareholder that already owns about a quarter of the company. What does that change?

SOFIA: It's halfway there already.

MARCO: A head start, not halfway. Strong answer: the bidder's side starts with about a quarter of the shares, which makes life hard for a rival, like the irrevocable at Steyr. It also knows the business better than any outsider, so the other shareholders should ask whether forty-six pence reflects what it knows. That's why only the independent directors give a view, and why they have to test the price against the football field, not just against Tuesday's close.
