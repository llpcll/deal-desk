# The Deal Desk, episode 08: Equity value versus enterprise value, and the price tag that lies
Microsoft paid sixty-eight point seven billion dollars for Activision Blizzard. Or did it? Week two starts with the most important distinction in valuation.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode eight. Week two. Last week was accounting. This week is value: what a company is worth, and how bankers measure it. And we start with the distinction that trips up more candidates than anything else.

SOFIA: Equity value and enterprise value.

MARCO: Exactly. And the story is Microsoft and Activision Blizzard. January eighteenth, twenty twenty-two. Microsoft announces it will buy Activision Blizzard, the company behind Call of Duty, World of Warcraft and Candy Crush. All cash. Ninety-five dollars a share. That was roughly forty-five percent above where Activision's shares had closed the previous trading day.

SOFIA: And the headlines said sixty-nine billion.

MARCO: Sixty-eight point seven billion dollars. That's the number in Microsoft's press release. But here's the detail almost everyone skipped. The release said sixty-eight point seven billion, inclusive of Activision Blizzard's net cash.

SOFIA: What does that mean?

MARCO: If you take ninety-five dollars and multiply it by all of Activision's shares, you get a number somewhere north of seventy billion dollars. That's what Activision's shareholders were actually going to receive. But Activision was sitting on billions of dollars of cash, more than its debt. Take that net cash away, and you get sixty-eight point seven.

SOFIA: So which one was the price?

MARCO: Both, depending on the question you're asking. The seventy-plus is the equity value: what the shareholders get. The sixty-eight point seven is closer to the enterprise value: what Microsoft was really paying for the business itself, because it got Activision's cash pile along with it.

SOFIA: And then the deal took ages.

MARCO: Nearly two years. The American competition regulator, the F-T-C, sued to block it and lost in court. The British regulator, the C-M-A, blocked it in April twenty twenty-three, worried about Microsoft dominating cloud gaming. Microsoft restructured the deal, selling the cloud streaming rights for Activision's games outside the European Economic Area to Ubisoft, and the C-M-A cleared it. It closed on the thirteenth of October, twenty twenty-three. But today's lesson is in the first press release.

SOFIA: Okay. So, from scratch. What's equity value?

MARCO: The value of the company to its shareholders. For a listed company, it's simple: share price times the number of shares. Also called market capitalisation, or market cap. Strictly, bankers use diluted shares, counting options and other securities that could turn into shares, but ignore that for now.

SOFIA: And enterprise value?

MARCO: The value of the operating business to everyone who has funded it. Shareholders and lenders. The simple version: equity value, plus debt, minus cash.

SOFIA: Why plus debt and minus cash?

MARCO: Think of buying a house. A house worth five hundred thousand, a mortgage of three hundred thousand. The owner's equity is two hundred. If you buy the house, you pay the owner two hundred and take over the three hundred mortgage. So the house itself cost you five hundred. That's enterprise value: equity plus debt.

SOFIA: And the cash?

MARCO: Now say the owner left fifty thousand in a drawer, and it comes with the house. You'd pay the owner two hundred and fifty. But the house itself is still worth five hundred. The cash doesn't make it a better house. So to value the house, you take the cash out. Equity two hundred and fifty, plus debt three hundred, minus cash fifty. Five hundred.

SOFIA: Okay. But if a company has more cash, it's worth more. Its E-V should go up, surely.

MARCO: That's the trap. It's the one interviewers use most on this topic. Say a company raises a billion by issuing new shares and keeps the money. Equity value goes up by a billion. Cash goes up by a billion. Enterprise value: unchanged. The factories didn't change, the customers didn't change.

SOFIA: And if it borrows a billion and keeps the cash?

MARCO: Debt up a billion, cash up a billion. They cancel. E-V unchanged again. And this time equity value is unchanged too, because shareholders haven't gained anything: they've got a billion more cash and a billion more debt. Enterprise value only changes when the business itself changes, or when the market's view of it changes.

SOFIA: Why do bankers even bother with enterprise value? The shareholders care about equity.

MARCO: Because when you compare companies, you want to compare businesses, not how they're financed. Two identical supermarket chains. One funded mostly with debt, one with shares. Their equity values look completely different. Their enterprise values should be about the same. So E-V lets you compare like with like.

SOFIA: Which leads to multiples.

MARCO: Right, and this is where the second trap lives. Enterprise value belongs to everyone: lenders and shareholders. So you pair it with numbers that also belong to everyone, before interest is paid. Revenue, EBITDA, EBIT. Equity value belongs to shareholders only, so you pair it with numbers that belong to shareholders only, after interest. Net income, earnings per share.

SOFIA: So E-V over EBITDA is fine, and share price over earnings per share, the P-E ratio, is fine. But E-V over net income...

MARCO: Is a mismatch. You'd be comparing what everyone owns with profit that's already been paid to the lenders. Say that in an interview and a banker will notice immediately.

SOFIA: Okay. And another thing. Microsoft paid ninety-five dollars a share. So a company with a share price of ninety-five is worth more than one with a price of twenty?

MARCO: Third trap. Share price on its own tells you nothing about size. A company with a ninety-five dollar share price and ten million shares has an equity value of under one billion. A company with a twenty dollar price and five billion shares is worth a hundred billion. Always multiply by the share count.

SOFIA: Last one. Can enterprise value be negative?

MARCO: Yes. If a company has more cash than its equity value plus its debt. It happens with small companies the market has given up on, often in biotech: the market thinks the business will burn through its cash. Negative E-V is the market saying the operations are worth less than nothing.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what's the difference between equity value and enterprise value?

SOFIA: Equity value is what shareholders own, and enterprise value is equity plus debt minus cash.

MARCO: That's the formula. Give the meaning too. Equity value is the value of the company to its shareholders only. Enterprise value is the value of the core operating business to all capital providers, both debt and equity. You get from one to the other by adding debt and subtracting cash, plus a few other items we'll cover tomorrow.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Why do you subtract cash?

SOFIA: Because cash isn't part of the business?

MARCO: Close. Make it sharper. Because cash is a non-operating asset. A buyer effectively gets it back when they acquire the company, and it doesn't generate the business's operating profits. So it reduces the true cost of buying the business.

SOFIA: And third?

MARCO: Third is the curveball. A company issues one hundred of new debt and uses it to pay a dividend to shareholders. What happens to equity value and enterprise value?

SOFIA: Debt goes up one hundred. Cash doesn't change, because it went straight out. Equity value... goes down one hundred?

MARCO: Right, in theory. The cash left the company, so each share is worth less. Equity value down one hundred, debt up one hundred, cash unchanged. Enterprise value unchanged. That's exactly what private equity owners do in something called a dividend recap. We'll see it in the L-B-O weeks.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: Yesterday, Transom Capital Group, a private equity firm, agreed to buy SoundThinking, an American public safety technology company. Its best-known product is ShotSpotter, which detects gunshots with microphones and alerts police.

SOFIA: How much?

MARCO: Eight dollars a share in cash up front. That's about forty-six percent above the previous day's close. On top of that, shareholders get what's called a contingent value right, a C-V-R, worth up to three dollars more per share, paid only if two of its products hit revenue targets in twenty twenty-seven.

SOFIA: So the price isn't fixed.

MARCO: Exactly. And look how the company states the value: an enterprise value of about one hundred and fourteen million dollars on the upfront cash alone, or about one hundred and fifty-nine million if the maximum C-V-R is paid.

SOFIA: So even at deal level, they talk in enterprise value.

MARCO: Always. Here's your interview question: why would a buyer and seller use a C-V-R?

SOFIA: Because they disagree on what the company's worth?

MARCO: That's the core. They disagree about the future. The seller's board thinks revenue will grow. The buyer isn't sure. A C-V-R bridges the gap: the buyer pays more only if the growth actually happens. Pharmaceutical deals use them all the time, tied to drug approvals.

SOFIA: Okay. Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: market cap five hundred, debt two hundred, cash fifty. What's the enterprise value? Question two: the company borrows one hundred and keeps the cash. What happens to enterprise value? Question three: share price twenty, fifty shares. What's the equity value? Question four: which value goes with the P-E ratio, equity value or enterprise value? Question five: equity value one hundred, no debt, cash one hundred and fifty. What's the enterprise value?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: five hundred plus two hundred minus fifty, six hundred and fifty. Two: nothing. Debt and cash both rise by one hundred. Three: one thousand. Four: equity value. Earnings belong to shareholders. Five: minus fifty. The market values the business at less than the cash it holds.

SOFIA: The negative one is still strange.

MARCO: It should feel strange. Tomorrow we go deeper into the bridge between equity and enterprise value: minority interests, preferred shares and leases. The bits that separate a good candidate from a great one.
