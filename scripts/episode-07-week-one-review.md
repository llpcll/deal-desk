# The Deal Desk, episode 07: Week one review, no notes allowed
Six episodes, one quiz. How banks are organised, the three statements, and working capital, tested the way an interviewer would.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode seven. Review day. No new story today. Instead, I'm going to put Sofia through the whole of week one, the way an interviewer would, and we'll fix the answers as we go. If you're listening, answer in your head before she does.

SOFIA: No pressure, then.

MARCO: None at all. Round one, how a bank works. What's the difference between coverage and product teams?

SOFIA: Coverage teams are organised by industry and own the client relationship. Product teams, like mergers and acquisitions, E-C-M and D-C-M, bring the specialist execution when a deal happens.

MARCO: Good. Why is there a wall between the investment banking division and sales and trading?

SOFIA: Because bankers have confidential information about deals, and traders trade on public information. If they shared it, you'd get insider trading.

MARCO: Right. Private side and public side. In the Kraft Heinz merger, who made the final decision on the deal: Centerview or Kraft's board?

SOFIA: The board. The bankers advise. They might give a fairness opinion, but the board decides.

MARCO: And why might a board hire an elite boutique rather than a bulge bracket bank?

SOFIA: Because boutiques only advise, so there are no conflicts from lending or trading, and you get very senior bankers on the deal.

MARCO: That's round one done, no mistakes. Round two, the income statement. Walk me through it.

SOFIA: Revenue, minus cost of goods sold, gives gross profit. Minus operating expenses like S-G-and-A and R-and-D, gives operating income, or EBIT. Minus interest gives pre-tax income, minus tax gives net income.

MARCO: Good. You've fixed the order since Thursday. Where's EBITDA in that?

SOFIA: It's not usually a line on the statement. You get it by adding depreciation and amortisation back to EBIT.

MARCO: And is EBITDA the same as cash flow?

SOFIA: No. It ignores capex, changes in working capital, interest and tax. A company can have positive EBITDA and still burn cash.

MARCO: And what was wrong with WeWork's community-adjusted EBITDA?

SOFIA: It stripped out costs like marketing and head office that come back every year. That's not adjusting for one-offs, that's removing the business model.

MARCO: Perfect. Round three, the balance sheet. The equation.

SOFIA: Assets equal liabilities plus equity.

MARCO: Why must it balance?

SOFIA: Because every asset was paid for somehow, either with money someone lent, which is a liability, or with the owners' money or retained profits, which is equity.

MARCO: Good. Now a trap. A company has large retained earnings. Does it have lots of cash?

SOFIA: Not necessarily. Retained earnings is accumulated profit kept in the business, but it might have been spent on equipment or used to repay debt.

MARCO: Right. And what did Lehman's Repo one-oh-five actually do?

SOFIA: It treated short-term borrowing as a sale of assets at quarter end, used the cash to pay down debt, and made leverage look lower. Then it bought the assets back a few days later.

MARCO: Good. A bank has a thousand of assets and fifty of equity. What fall in asset values wipes out the equity?

SOFIA: Five percent. That's twenty times leverage.

MARCO: Round four, the cash flow statement. Three sections?

SOFIA: Operating, investing and financing.

MARCO: Net income is eighty, depreciation twenty, receivables go up by fifteen. Cash from operations?

SOFIA: Eighty plus twenty minus fifteen. Eighty-five.

MARCO: Correct. Free cash flow, if capex is fifty?

SOFIA: Thirty-five. Cash from operations minus capex.

MARCO: Why did Enron need the prepay deals?

SOFIA: Because mark-to-market accounting inflated profits, but the cash didn't follow. The prepays brought in borrowed money disguised as operating cash flow, so the cash looked like it matched the profits.

MARCO: Very good. Round five. The big one. Depreciation goes up by ten, tax rate twenty-five percent. Go.

SOFIA: Income statement: operating income down ten, tax down two point five, net income down seven point five. Cash flow statement: net income down seven point five, add back ten of depreciation, cash up two point five. Balance sheet: cash up two point five, P-P-and-E down ten, so assets down seven point five. Retained earnings down seven point five, so equity down seven point five. Balances.

MARCO: Twenty-five seconds. That's interview speed. Now the variation. The company buys one hundred of equipment with cash.

SOFIA: Income statement: nothing. It's not an expense yet. Cash flow: minus one hundred in investing. Balance sheet: cash down one hundred, P-P-and-E up one hundred. Total assets unchanged.

MARCO: And a harder one. The company records twenty of revenue, but the customer hasn't paid. Tax at twenty-five percent.

SOFIA: Revenue up twenty, tax up five, net income up fifteen. Cash flow: net income up fifteen, receivables up twenty so minus twenty, cash down five. Balance sheet: cash down five, receivables up twenty, assets up fifteen. Retained earnings up fifteen. Balances.

MARCO: And why is cash down?

SOFIA: Because we owe tax on the profit, but we haven't collected any cash yet.

MARCO: Excellent. Round six, working capital. Define operating working capital.

SOFIA: Receivables plus inventory minus payables.

MARCO: Inventory days thirty, receivable days twenty, payable days sixty. Cash conversion cycle?

SOFIA: Thirty plus twenty minus sixty. Minus ten days.

MARCO: Is negative working capital always a bad sign?

SOFIA: No. For Amazon or supermarkets, it means customers pay before suppliers are paid, which is a strength. But it can also mean a company can't pay its suppliers, so you check why.

MARCO: Last one in this round. A company grows fifty percent, it's profitable, but its cash falls. Why?

SOFIA: Growth ties up cash in receivables and inventory before the profit turns into cash. Growing broke.

MARCO: That's a clean week. Now, one thing I want you to notice. The mistakes you made on the first days were almost all about order and direction. Forgetting tax. Thinking depreciation is cash. Thinking receivables going up is good for cash. Those are exactly the mistakes interviewers are listening for. Fix those, and the technical part of a spring week interview stops being scary.

SOFIA: Okay. So what should someone do with this before an interview?

MARCO: Say the answers out loud. Walking through the three statements is a spoken skill, not a written one. Do the depreciation question every day until it takes under thirty seconds. And have one deal ready to talk about, which brings us to today's.

SOFIA: Deal of the day.

MARCO: And this one's close to home for you. Last Friday, Eaton, the Irish-domiciled power management group, agreed to buy COL Group, an Italian company, from Oaktree, the investment firm. Eight hundred and ten million euros of enterprise value.

SOFIA: Italian where?

MARCO: Four sites. Turin, Milan, Bergamo and Catania. About four hundred employees. COL makes medium-voltage electrical distribution equipment, including switchgear that doesn't use S-F-six, a greenhouse gas that's been used for decades to insulate electrical equipment. And grid automation and modular power systems.

SOFIA: Why does Eaton want it?

MARCO: The same reason we saw with Rexel yesterday. Data centres and the power grid. Everyone building A-I data centres needs to get power from the grid to the chips, and European utilities are upgrading their networks. Eaton wants more manufacturing capacity in Europe to serve both. Closing is expected in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

SOFIA: How big is COL?

MARCO: Eaton expects revenue of about two hundred and fifty million euros in twenty twenty-seven. So here's your interview question. What's the rough multiple, and how would you talk about it?

SOFIA: Eight hundred and ten over two hundred and fifty. About three point two times revenue.

MARCO: Right. On forward revenue. And then the comment. For an equipment manufacturer, that's a meaningful price, which tells you the buyer is paying for growth and for scarce manufacturing capacity in a market where demand is running ahead of supply. And remember the lesson from Urbaser: eight hundred and ten million is enterprise value. What Oaktree takes home as equity depends on the debt inside COL.

SOFIA: And Oaktree is a client of the bank's financial sponsors group.

MARCO: Exactly. You've just used three episodes in one answer. That's what interviewers want.

SOFIA: Quiz time?

MARCO: Review quiz, so it's longer. Ten questions. For you listening. Tax at twenty-five percent where relevant. One: which team owns the long-term client relationship? Two: EBITDA is two hundred, depreciation fifty, interest thirty. What's net income? Three: why add depreciation back on the cash flow statement? Four: a company issues fifty of new shares for cash. What changes on the balance sheet? Five: a company repays forty of debt with cash. What happens to net income? Six: depreciation goes up by twenty. What happens to cash? Seven: receivables fall by ten. What happens to cash? Eight: what's the difference between book value and market value of equity? Nine: name one legitimate adjustment to EBITDA. Ten: a buyer pays eight hundred million for a business with two hundred million of revenue. What's the revenue multiple?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: coverage. Two: EBIT one hundred and fifty, pre-tax income one hundred and twenty, tax thirty, net income ninety. Three: because it reduced net income but no cash left the business in that period. Four: cash up fifty, equity up fifty. Five: nothing. Repaying debt isn't an expense. Only interest hits the income statement. Six: cash up five, because of the tax saving. Seven: cash up ten, customers paid. Eight: book value is the accounting value on the balance sheet, market value is share price times number of shares, and it reflects expected future earnings. Nine: a genuine one-off cost, like a legal settlement or restructuring charge, as long as it doesn't come back every year. Ten: four times revenue.

SOFIA: I'd have got question five wrong a week ago.

MARCO: That's the point. Week two starts tomorrow: value. Equity value versus enterprise value, told through Microsoft's sixty-eight point seven billion dollar purchase of Activision Blizzard, and why the headline price wasn't really the price.
