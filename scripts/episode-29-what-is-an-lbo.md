# The Deal Desk, episode 29: What a leveraged buyout is, or how K-K-R borrowed its way to R-J-R Nabisco
In nineteen eighty-eight a tobacco and biscuit company went for about twenty-five billion dollars, most of it borrowed. Then a New York private equity firm agrees to buy Ashtead Technology, with a term loan behind its offer.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-nine. Week five, and a new kind of buyer. Not a company looking for synergies, but a fund that buys a business with borrowed money and plans to sell it again in a few years. The leveraged buyout, the L-B-O. And we start thirty-eight years ago this week, in New York.

SOFIA: Barbarians at the Gate.

MARCO: That's the book. On Thursday the twentieth of October nineteen eighty-eight, Ross Johnson, the chief executive of R-J-R Nabisco, said he was leading a group, backed by Shearson Lehman Hutton, that was considering taking the company private. Seventy-five dollars a share, about sixteen point nine billion dollars. R-J-R made Winston and Camel cigarettes, and Oreo and Ritz biscuits.

SOFIA: The boss buying his own company.

MARCO: A management buyout, yes. And the market didn't believe seventy-five was the end. The shares jumped more than twenty-one dollars, to over seventy-seven.

SOFIA: Above the offer. Round five from yesterday: the market expects a higher bid.

MARCO: And it came. The next Monday, the twenty-fourth, Kohlberg Kravis Roberts, K-K-R, offered ninety dollars a share, about twenty point three billion. Then came an auction that ran through November. In the final round, Johnson's group bid one hundred and twelve dollars a share. K-K-R bid one hundred and nine.

SOFIA: So Johnson won.

MARCO: K-K-R won. The board judged its offer more certain. Johnson's bid lacked what was called a reset, a promise to adjust the securities in the offer so they'd be worth what was claimed, so his one hundred and twelve could have turned out to be worth less. And many directors were uneasy about the golden parachute Johnson had arranged for himself.

SOFIA: How big was it in the end?

MARCO: About twenty-five billion dollars for the shares, and about thirty-one billion including the debt that came with the company. The largest leveraged buyout in history, a record that stood for more than seventeen years. And most of the money was borrowed.

SOFIA: Borrowed by K-K-R?

MARCO: Hold that question, because it's the first trap. So what is a leveraged buyout? Round numbers, millions of pounds. A private equity fund wants to buy Harbour, a company with EBITDA of one hundred a year. It pays ten times, so one thousand for the whole business. To keep it simple, Harbour has no debt today.

SOFIA: So the fund writes a cheque for one thousand.

MARCO: It writes a cheque for four hundred. That's the equity. The other six hundred is borrowed, six times EBITDA, from banks and bond investors.

SOFIA: So the fund owes six hundred.

MARCO: First trap. The fund doesn't owe it. The fund sets up a new company, a bidco, which borrows the six hundred and buys Harbour. After the deal the debt sits with Harbour, and Harbour's own cash pays the interest and repays it. If it all goes wrong, the fund can lose its four hundred, but the lenders can't come after the fund for the rest. That's what leveraged means here: the company carries the borrowing that paid for it.

MARCO: Now five years pass, and Harbour does two things. It grows: EBITDA rises from one hundred to one hundred and thirty. And it pays down debt. After interest, tax and investment, it has sixty a year of spare cash, three hundred over five years, and all of it goes to repay the loans. Debt falls from six hundred to three hundred. Then the fund sells.

SOFIA: At ten times again?

MARCO: Assume so. Ten times one hundred and thirty, one thousand three hundred for the business. Take off the three hundred of debt still owed. What's left for the fund?

SOFIA: One thousand. From four hundred.

MARCO: Two and a half times its money in five years. Where did the six hundred of gain come from?

SOFIA: From selling for more than it paid.

MARCO: Second trap, half right. The multiple didn't change, so none of it came from selling at a richer price. Three hundred came from EBITDA growth, thirty more times ten. The other three hundred came from repaying debt: every pound of debt Harbour repays is a pound more for the equity. Growth, debt paydown and a change in the multiple. Those are the three sources of return in an L-B-O, and a careful buyer plans on the first two and treats the third as luck.

SOFIA: And what does the debt add? Harbour would have grown anyway.

MARCO: Compare a buyer that pays the whole thousand in cash. Same Harbour, same growth. It ends with the business, worth one thousand three hundred, plus the cash Harbour piled up, a little over three hundred because there's no interest to pay. Roughly one point six times its money. The gain in pounds is similar, but the cheque was two and a half times bigger.

SOFIA: Then why not borrow everything? Nine hundred of debt and one hundred of equity.

MARCO: Third trap. The debt has to be served. Six hundred at eight percent is forty-eight a year of interest, before Harbour repays a pound. Nine hundred would be seventy-two, out of EBITDA of one hundred, before tax and investment. One bad year and Harbour can't pay. And the arithmetic works in reverse too. If Harbour's value falls to six hundred and the debt is still six hundred, the fund's equity is worth nothing. The all-cash buyer still has six hundred out of its thousand. Leverage magnifies gains and losses alike.

SOFIA: Did that happen to R-J-R?

MARCO: Not to zero. But the debt weighed on the business, R-J-R sold off parts, from Del Monte to its European biscuit businesses, and in nineteen ninety K-K-R put in about one point seven billion dollars of new equity to shore it up. The biggest buyout of its time isn't remembered as one of the best. More on that in episode thirty-three.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: walk me through a leveraged buyout.

SOFIA: A private equity firm borrows a lot of money, buys a company, and sells it later for more.

MARCO: The outline is there, but the firm doesn't borrow it, and selling for more is the hope, not the plan. Strong answer: a financial sponsor buys a company with some equity from its fund and more debt, raised against the target's own cash flows and owed by the target. For a few years the company's cash pays the interest and repays debt, while the sponsor works on growing EBITDA. Then it exits: a sale to a company or another fund, or an I-P-O. The return comes from growth, debt paydown and any change in the multiple.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: What makes a good L-B-O candidate?

SOFIA: A company that's growing fast, so there's plenty of upside.

MARCO: Growth helps, but fast growth usually eats cash, and lenders want cash. Strong answer: steady, predictable cash flows that can carry the debt, so usually a mature business in a stable market. Modest investment needs, little debt today, costs to cut or divisions to sell, a management team worth backing, and a believable exit. R-J-R had the first one in abundance: people kept buying cigarettes and biscuits, whatever the economy did.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: An L-B-O, announced on Wednesday the twenty-first of October. Ashtead Technology, a British company listed on the AIM market, supplies subsea equipment and services to the offshore energy industry. It has a fleet of more than thirty thousand assets, and about eighty-five percent of them can be used both for oil and gas and for offshore renewables. It has agreed to be bought by Ember, a New York private equity firm founded in twenty eighteen by a team from Global Infrastructure Partners.

SOFIA: How much?

MARCO: Six hundred and fifteen pence a share in cash, about five hundred and five million pounds for the shares, fully diluted. That's eighty point nine percent over the close of three hundred and forty pence on the twenty-second of September, the last business day before the offer period began. And fifty-nine point three percent over the three-month volume-weighted average.

SOFIA: An eighty percent premium. Ember must have wanted it badly.

MARCO: It took four proposals, and the board rejected the first two. Now, five hundred and five million is the price of what?

SOFIA: The equity. Episode eight, every week.

MARCO: Every week. Ashtead had net debt of one hundred and sixteen point seven million pounds at the end of June. Add that and you get an enterprise value of roughly six hundred and twenty million. That's my arithmetic, not a figure in the announcement.

SOFIA: Where's the leverage?

MARCO: In the financing. The cash comes from two places: equity from Ember's funds, including a passive co-investment from CalPERS, the Californian public pension fund, and a term loan under an interim facilities agreement, arranged by Santander. The buyer is a new company, Pine Acquisition U-K Bidco.

SOFIA: A bidco. Like Harbour.

MARCO: Exactly like Harbour. The announcement doesn't say how big the loan is, so we can't say how leveraged the deal will be. What we do know: as a listed company Ashtead ran with modest debt, leverage of one point four times EBITDA at the end of June.

SOFIA: Why is the loan called interim?

MARCO: That's your interview question. Strong answer: under the British Takeover Code, a cash bidder must show the money is there before it announces a firm offer, and its financial adviser must confirm it. Here that's Santander. Interim facilities are short, simple loans the banks commit to on day one, so the cash is certain. Around completion, the sponsor usually replaces them with the long-term debt it wants. Certainty first, then the best terms.

SOFIA: And will it happen?

MARCO: The board intends to recommend it unanimously. It still needs shareholders, a court and regulators, and completion is expected late in twenty twenty-six or early in twenty twenty-seven. On Wednesday morning the shares rose about fifteen percent, to just over six hundred pence, a little under the offer. The market thinks it will close.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: a fund buys Harbour for one thousand, with six hundred of debt. Five years later it sells for one thousand three hundred, with three hundred of debt left. What multiple of its money does the fund make? Question two: in a leveraged buyout, who owes the acquisition debt? Question three: what do lenders most want to see in an L-B-O target? Question four: why did R-J-R's board choose K-K-R's one hundred and nine dollars over management's one hundred and twelve? Question five: Ember pays about five hundred and five million pounds for Ashtead's shares, and Ashtead's net debt is about one hundred and seventeen million. Roughly what is the enterprise value?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: two and a half times. One thousand three hundred minus three hundred is one thousand, on four hundred of equity. Two: the company being bought, through the bidco. The fund can lose its equity, but no more. Three: steady, predictable cash flows that can pay the interest and repay the debt. Four: K-K-R's offer was more certain. Management's had no reset, so its one hundred and twelve might have been worth less. Five: about six hundred and twenty million pounds, the equity value plus the net debt.

SOFIA: So the company carries the debt, its cash pays it off, and the fund keeps what's left.

MARCO: And how good that is depends on how long it takes. Tomorrow, episode thirty: the internal rate of return and the money multiple, the two numbers every private equity fund is judged by, and why Harbour's two and a half times looks different over three years and over seven.
