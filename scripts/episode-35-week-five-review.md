# The Deal Desk, episode 35: Week five review, from the bidco to the exit
In twenty twenty two buyout firms beat Kone to thyssenkrupp's lifts, and this year Kone came back to buy them. Six episodes of private equity, tested the way an interviewer would. Then Providence agrees to buy Fitness Park, the French gym chain, at a price nobody will confirm.

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode thirty-five. Review day. Week five was private equity: what a leveraged buyout is, the I-R-R and the money multiple, the debt structure, the paper L-B-O, buyouts gone wrong, and an Italian deal taken apart. Today I test Sofia on all of it, no notes. If you're listening, answer before she does.

SOFIA: No notes. I'm used to it by now.

MARCO: One story first, and it runs through the whole week. On the twenty-seventh of February twenty twenty, thyssenkrupp, the German steel and engineering group, agreed to sell its lift business to a consortium led by Advent and Cinven, two buyout firms, with the R-A-G foundation. Seventeen point two billion euros. Thyssenkrupp reinvested one point two five billion of it for a stake.

SOFIA: A carve-out. And the funds set up a bidco and borrowed against the lifts.

MARCO: And there was another bidder. Kone, the Finnish lift maker, had made an offer roughly matching the price, then dropped out over the risk that competition authorities would block it. The deal closed in July twenty twenty, and the business became TK Elevator.

SOFIA: So the funds won by being more certain. Like K-K-R at R-J-R.

MARCO: Exactly. Now jump to the twenty-ninth of April this year. Kone came back and agreed to buy TK Elevator from the funds. An enterprise value of twenty-nine point four billion euros. Five billion in cash at closing, two hundred and seventy million new Kone shares worth about fifteen point two billion, and about nine point two billion of net debt that Kone takes on.

SOFIA: Seventeen point two to twenty-nine point four. So the funds made about seventy percent.

MARCO: First trap, and it's the week in one sentence. Both figures are enterprise values. The funds own the equity, and the debt comes off first. Add up what Kone pays the sellers: five in cash plus fifteen point two in shares, about twenty billion for the equity. How much equity did the funds put in back in twenty twenty?

SOFIA: Seventeen point two minus whatever they borrowed.

MARCO: Right method, but nobody has published that number, so we can't work out the return. We only know that with debt underneath, the equity gains a bigger percentage than the business. Which other episodes are in this story?

SOFIA: Thirty, because it took six years, and the I-R-R depends on time. Thirty-one, because nine point two billion of debt is still there. And Kone is a company, so it has synergies.

MARCO: Seven hundred million euros a year of cost savings, at the full run rate. And the sellers take most of the price in shares, about a third of Kone's shares, so the funds haven't fully exited yet. Schindler, a rival, says it will challenge the deal with the competition authorities, and closing is expected in the second quarter of twenty twenty-seven at the earliest. Now the week's other stories, one line each. Hilton, Boots and Toys R Us?

SOFIA: Hilton: three times the money, over eleven years. Boots: the banks couldn't sell the loans. Toys R Us: the cash fell and the debt stayed.

MARCO: Round one, returns. Harbour, millions of pounds. Bought at ten times EBITDA of one hundred, so one thousand: six hundred of debt, four hundred of equity. Five years later EBITDA is one hundred and fifty and the debt is down to four hundred. But buyers now pay only eight times. What does the fund get?

SOFIA: Eight times one hundred and fifty is one thousand two hundred. Minus four hundred of debt: eight hundred. Twice its money, so twenty percent a year.

MARCO: Second trap. That's simple interest. Twenty percent compounded for five years is about two and a half times. Doubling in five years is about fifteen percent.

SOFIA: Fifteen. And the four hundred of gain all came from growth, because EBITDA grew by half.

MARCO: Split it into the three sources. Growth: fifty more EBITDA at the entry multiple of ten, five hundred. Debt paydown: two hundred. The multiple: two turns lower on one hundred and fifty, minus three hundred. Five hundred plus two hundred minus three hundred: four hundred. The fall in the multiple took away more than half of what growth added.

SOFIA: Which is why you never plan on the multiple.

MARCO: Round two, the debt structure. The six hundred is four hundred of senior secured loans and two hundred of bonds. A bad few years, and the business is worth four hundred and fifty. Who gets what?

SOFIA: The senior lenders get their four hundred. The bonds get fifty, twenty-five pence in the pound. The equity gets nothing. And the fulcrum is the senior lenders, because they're the ones in charge.

MARCO: Third trap. Right numbers, wrong label. The fulcrum is the most senior layer that isn't paid in full. The senior lenders are covered, so it's the bondholders, and in a restructuring they'd usually swap their debt for the equity.

MARCO: Round three, the paper L-B-O. Five years of EBITDA adding up to five hundred and ninety, fifty of depreciation, two hundred and forty of interest, tax at twenty-five percent. How much tax?

SOFIA: On profit, not on EBITDA. Five hundred and ninety minus fifty minus two hundred and forty is three hundred. Seventy-five of tax.

MARCO: Round four, buyouts gone wrong. Six hundred of debt, and EBITDA falls from one hundred to eighty. The loans have a covenant: leverage no higher than seven times. Harbour is still paying every pound of interest.

SOFIA: Six hundred over eighty: seven and a half times. Over the limit, but it pays its interest, so it can wait for EBITDA to recover.

MARCO: Fourth trap. A covenant breach is a default, missed payment or not, and the lenders can demand their money back. So the sponsor goes to them before the test date: a waiver or an amendment, for a fee and a higher rate, or an equity cure, new money from the fund. And the equity, if buyers pay nine times?

SOFIA: Nine times eighty is seven hundred and twenty, minus six hundred: one hundred and twenty. EBITDA fell a fifth, the equity seventy percent.

MARCO: Round five, taking a deal apart. Undisturbed price twenty euros, rumours take it to twenty-four, the offer is twenty-five. Premium?

SOFIA: Twenty-five percent, against the undisturbed twenty. And the multiple goes on enterprise value, never the equity value.

MARCO: So where did you slip?

SOFIA: Enterprise value as the funds' gain. Simple interest, again. The fulcrum. And thinking paying the interest was enough.

MARCO: Four slips, one mistake: reading a number without asking who stands ahead of you. The equity is what's left after the debt, and a covenant is a promise on top of the payments.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: who pays more for a business, a private equity fund or a company?

SOFIA: The fund. It borrows more, so it can pay more.

MARCO: Debt changes who funds the price, not what the business is worth. Strong answer: usually the company, because it brings synergies, while a fund has to earn its return on the business as it stands. Ahold Delhaize, from episode thirty-two, paid an effective five times thanks to synergies. But a fund can win on certainty. In twenty twenty Kone's offer for the lifts roughly matched the price, and the funds won because Kone carried the competition risk.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: A fund sells a portfolio company and takes most of the price in the buyer's shares. Why accept, and what's the risk?

SOFIA: Because the buyer didn't have the cash.

MARCO: Maybe, but answer from the fund's side. Strong answer: shares let the fund keep a part of the synergies it's selling, and let the buyer pay more without borrowing it all. The risks: the investment isn't cash yet, so the I-R-R waits. The share price can fall, and a big block can't be sold at once without pushing it down. So it sells in stages, like Blackstone with Hilton.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: The five questions, on a deal with almost no numbers. On Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October, Providence Equity Partners, an American private equity firm, agreed to buy Fitness Park, the French low-cost gym chain. More than one point seven million members and more than four hundred and sixty clubs, most of them in France.

SOFIA: Question one, who and why. A fund that wants a return.

MARCO: And a plan. Providence bought VivaGym, in Spain and Portugal, in twenty twenty-four, when it had about sixty gyms. With the Synergym deal agreed in April, about four hundred and fifty. Add Fitness Park: more than nine hundred. That's buy and build, a platform and then add-ons. The sellers are the founder, Philippe Herbette, who stays as chairman, and three investors, including Future French Champions, backed by Qatar's sovereign fund and Bpifrance.

SOFIA: Question two, the price.

MARCO: Not disclosed. In September the Spanish newspaper Expansión put the valuation at about six hundred million euros. Nobody has confirmed it, or said whether it's equity or enterprise value.

SOFIA: Network revenue was four hundred and sixty-one million last year. So six hundred is about one point three times sales. Cheap.

MARCO: Fifth trap. Network revenue includes the sales of the franchised clubs, and most of that money belongs to the franchisees. Fitness Park earns fees on it. Divide a price by someone else's revenue and any deal looks cheap. You'd want the company's own revenue and EBITDA, and neither has been published.

SOFIA: Question three. How is it paid for?

MARCO: Not disclosed either. But last year Fitness Park took a two hundred and eighty million euro financing package from Carlyle Global Credit, to refinance debt and fund openings. Episode thirty-four: existing debt usually falls due when control changes, so it goes in the uses.

SOFIA: Question four. Does it work for Providence?

MARCO: Episode twenty-nine. Members pay every month, which lenders like. But Herbette has talked of up to one hundred openings a year, and openings eat cash. Franchising helps: company-run clubs are meant to be about a quarter of the network, so franchisees pay for most of the rest.

SOFIA: Question five. What could stop it?

MARCO: The competition authorities. Providence has notified France and Morocco, and completion is subject to regulatory approvals.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: a fund buys Harbour at ten times EBITDA of one hundred, with six hundred of debt, and sells five years later at eight times EBITDA of one hundred and fifty, with four hundred of debt. What multiple of its money, and roughly what I-R-R? Question two: in that deal, how much did the fall in the multiple cost the fund? Question three: Harbour is worth four hundred and fifty, with four hundred of senior loans and two hundred of bonds. What do the bondholders get, and who is the fulcrum? Question four: Harbour's leverage is seven and a half times against a covenant of seven, and it pays all its interest. Is it in default? Question five: why shouldn't you divide Fitness Park's reported price by its network revenue?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: two times, and about fifteen percent, not twenty. Two: three hundred, two turns on one hundred and fifty of EBITDA. Three: fifty, twenty-five pence in the pound, and the bondholders are the fulcrum. Four: yes. A covenant breach is a default even with no missed payment, so the sponsor asks for a waiver or puts in an equity cure. Five: network revenue includes the franchisees' sales, and the six hundred million is unconfirmed, possibly equity, possibly enterprise value.
