# The Deal Desk, episode 36: Talking about a deal, or how to tell Microsoft and Activision in two minutes
In twenty twenty-two Microsoft agreed to pay sixty-eight point seven billion dollars for Activision Blizzard, and a British regulator nearly stopped it. Then Guidewire buys hyperexponential, a London insurance software firm, with four hundred million dollars of the price still to be earned.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode thirty-six. Almost every spring week interview has some version of one question: tell me about a deal you've followed. Today we build the answer. Two minutes, five questions and a view. We start with a deal most candidates have heard of, and few can tell well.

SOFIA: Which one?

MARCO: On the eighteenth of January twenty twenty-two, Microsoft agreed to buy Activision Blizzard, the company behind Call of Duty, World of Warcraft and Candy Crush. Ninety-five dollars a share, all in cash. Sixty-eight point seven billion dollars, including Activision's net cash.

SOFIA: And then the regulators.

MARCO: Then the regulators. On the twenty-sixth of April twenty twenty-three, the Competition and Markets Authority, the C-M-A, Britain's competition regulator, blocked it. Its concern in the end wasn't consoles. It was cloud gaming, where you stream a game instead of running it on your own machine.

SOFIA: Blocked. So how did it close?

MARCO: Microsoft changed the deal. In August twenty twenty-three it agreed to sell the cloud streaming rights to Activision's P-C and console games, current ones and any released over the next fifteen years, outside the European Economic Area, to Ubisoft, the French games publisher. The C-M-A approved the new deal on Friday the thirteenth of October twenty twenty-three, and Microsoft closed that same day.

SOFIA: Twenty-one months from signing to closing. I could talk about that for ten minutes.

MARCO: Which is the problem. You get two. Go on, tell it to me.

SOFIA: So, Microsoft had been building up its games business for years, and Xbox was behind Sony, and people were starting to play through subscriptions and the cloud, and then in January twenty twenty-two...

MARCO: First trap. Twenty seconds in, and the interviewer still doesn't know what the deal is. Chronology is how you remember a deal, not how you tell it. Lead with the headline, one sentence: who bought what, for how much, and when. Microsoft agreed to buy Activision Blizzard in January twenty twenty-two for sixty-eight point seven billion dollars in cash, and closed in October twenty twenty-three, after Britain nearly blocked it.

SOFIA: And then the five questions from episode thirty-four.

MARCO: Then the five questions: who and why, the price, the payment, whether it works, and what could stop it. Then a view, with one reason. Two minutes at a normal pace is about three hundred words. Fifteen seconds for the headline, about eighteen for each question, and the last fifteen for the view.

SOFIA: Eighteen seconds a question. That's not much.

MARCO: Which forces you to choose. One number per question, and one comparison for each number. Round numbers. Northgate, a listed company, agrees to buy Harbour. Harbour's shares were at ten euros before any rumours, and Northgate offers twelve, half in cash and half in its own shares. One hundred million Harbour shares, three hundred million euros of net debt, EBITDA of one hundred and fifty million. Northgate expects fifty million a year of cost savings, and listed peers trade at about eight times EBITDA. Give me the price.

SOFIA: Twelve euros a share, a twenty percent premium, one point two billion of equity value, one point five billion of enterprise value, ten times EBITDA, fifty million of synergies, half cash and half shares.

MARCO: Second trap. Every number is right, and the interviewer will remember none of them. That's a list. A number only means something next to another number. Ten times, against eight for the peers. That's two turns of EBITDA, three hundred million euros, paid for control and for the savings. Now add the fifty to the one hundred and fifty: Northgate pays one point five billion for two hundred of EBITDA. Seven and a half times. Below the peers.

SOFIA: So the price in one sentence: expensive on Harbour alone, cheap if the savings arrive.

MARCO: Exactly that. And it hands you your view. Is it a good deal?

SOFIA: Yes. Harbour is a good company. Steady EBITDA, a market leader.

MARCO: Third trap. A good company and a good deal aren't the same thing. At twenty euros a share Harbour would still be a good company, and a terrible deal. A view answers three things: good for whom, at this price, because of what. Say: good for Harbour's shareholders, twenty percent over the undisturbed price. For Northgate it works only if most of the fifty million arrives. And what I'd watch is the cost of getting there, because savings usually cost money to achieve.

SOFIA: And if I don't know what they cost?

MARCO: Then you say so, and you'll see why in the deal of the day.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First, the one we've been building: tell me about a deal you've followed.

SOFIA: Microsoft agreed to buy Activision Blizzard in January twenty twenty-two for sixty-eight point seven billion dollars in cash, and it closed in October twenty twenty-three after the C-M-A blocked it and Microsoft sold the cloud rights to Ubisoft. I think it was a good deal, because gaming is growing.

MARCO: A strong headline. But that view is about the industry, not the deal. Strong answer: after the headline, why. Content: nearly four hundred million monthly active players, enough, Microsoft said, to make it the third-largest gaming company by revenue, behind Tencent and Sony. The payment: all cash, ninety-five dollars a share. What nearly stopped it: the C-M-A, over cloud gaming, until the rights went to Ubisoft. Then the view, with one reason. The price was never the main risk; the regulator was. Microsoft got its deal by giving away the one piece the regulator cared about, and kept the games.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: The interviewer frowns and says: I think you're wrong, the regulator was right to worry. What do you do?

SOFIA: Agree with them. They know far more than I do.

MARCO: Folding is the weakest answer, and digging in is the next weakest. They're testing whether your view has a reason behind it. Strong answer: ask what they'd point to, and test your reason against it. If it survives, keep your view and say what would change your mind. You may be right about cloud gaming, but the remedy put the rights with a rival for fifteen years; if Ubisoft never used them, I'd change my mind.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: And a practice deal, small enough to tell in two minutes, with a London target. On Tuesday the twenty-seventh of October, Guidewire, the American company whose software runs the core systems of property and casualty insurers, agreed to buy hyperexponential, a London company that makes A-I software for pricing and underwriting commercial insurance.

SOFIA: Underwriting is deciding whether to insure a risk, and at what price.

MARCO: Right. Hyperexponential, which calls itself H-X, was founded in twenty seventeen. More than seventy-five billion dollars a year of commercial insurance premium is written through its platform, by customers including Aviva and Allianz. Guidewire's products are used by more than five hundred and seventy insurers in forty-four countries. Question one: who and why?

SOFIA: A software company buying a product its own customers want, so it can sell it to all five hundred and seventy of them.

MARCO: Good. Guidewire's phrase is agentic underwriting: A-I agents working beside underwriters and actuaries. And H-X was already a Guidewire partner, so each side knows the other's product. Question two.

SOFIA: The price is two point one billion dollars.

MARCO: Careful. It's one point seven billion dollars in cash, subject to adjustments, plus up to four hundred million more if H-X reaches certain recurring revenue targets. Two point one billion is the most it could cost. That's an earn-out.

SOFIA: Why would a seller accept that?

MARCO: Because buyer and seller disagree about growth. The seller says revenue will keep climbing, and the buyer says prove it. An earn-out lets both be right: the base price pays for what exists, the rest for what arrives. Here, nearly a fifth of the maximum price depends on results. Now, the multiple?

SOFIA: Revenue is probably about two hundred million dollars, so roughly eight times revenue.

MARCO: Fourth trap. Where did two hundred million come from?

SOFIA: It sounded about right.

MARCO: Neither company has disclosed H-X's revenue. Invent a number in an interview and the follow-up question will find it. Say it isn't disclosed, then say what you do know. In January twenty twenty-four H-X raised seventy-three million dollars, led by Battery Ventures, and said its sales had grown tenfold since twenty twenty-one. And Guidewire says H-X will add at least six and a half percentage points to its annual recurring revenue growth, its A-R-R, in its twenty twenty-seven financial year. Take Guidewire's A-R-R from its own results and you have a floor for H-X's.

SOFIA: Question three. How is it paid for?

MARCO: In cash. One billion dollars of new term loans, committed by Bank of America and Citibank, and the rest from Guidewire's own cash. Question four: Guidewire expects faster growth, within its long-term margin targets. Question five: closing is expected in the second quarter of Guidewire's twenty twenty-seven financial year.

SOFIA: So, in two minutes?

MARCO: Listen for the shape. Guidewire agreed on the twenty-seventh of October to buy hyperexponential, a London A-I pricing company, for one point seven billion dollars in cash, plus up to four hundred million on revenue targets. Why: to sell H-X to the insurers already on Guidewire. The price: revenue isn't disclosed, so no multiple, but the earn-out tells you the two sides disagreed on growth. The payment: mostly a billion of new loans. My view: sensible for Guidewire, because nearly a fifth of the maximum price waits for the growth. What I'd watch: whether the targets are met.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: Harbour's enterprise value is one point five billion euros, its EBITDA is one hundred and fifty million, and the buyer expects fifty million a year of savings. What multiple is the buyer effectively paying? Question two: what should the first sentence of a deal pitch say? Question three: an interviewer asks whether a deal was a good one. What does a strong view answer? Question four: Guidewire may pay up to two point one billion dollars for hyperexponential. What is the four hundred million on top of the base price? Question five: the interviewer asks for H-X's revenue, which hasn't been disclosed. What do you do?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: seven and a half times, one point five billion over two hundred of EBITDA including the savings, and ten times without them. Two: the headline. Who bought what, for how much, and when, not the background. Three: good for whom, at this price, and because of what. A good company isn't automatically a good deal. Four: an earn-out, paid only if H-X reaches recurring revenue targets. Five: say it isn't disclosed, then use what is, like Guidewire's six and a half points of extra A-R-R growth. Don't invent it.

SOFIA: So the headline first, one comparison per number, and a view I can defend.

MARCO: And tomorrow you defend a different kind of view. Episode thirty-seven: the markets pitch. Not a deal this time, but a call on a market, and how to make it in two minutes.
