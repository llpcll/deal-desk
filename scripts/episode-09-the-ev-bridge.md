# The Deal Desk, episode 09: The bridge from equity value to enterprise value
Ten point seven billion for the shares. Thirty-three point four billion for the company. Where did the other twenty-two billion go?

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode nine. Yesterday: equity value plus debt minus cash gives enterprise value. Today, the full version. Bankers call it the bridge. And I want to start with a deal where the bridge is enormous.

SOFIA: Which one?

MARCO: AES. An American power company: power plants, renewables, and two regulated utilities in Indiana and Ohio. In March this year, a consortium led by two infrastructure investors, Global Infrastructure Partners and E-Q-T, agreed to buy it for fifteen dollars a share in cash. Shareholders approved it in June.

SOFIA: And how big was the deal?

MARCO: Here's the interesting bit. The equity value, what shareholders receive, is about ten point seven billion dollars. The enterprise value is about thirty-three point four billion.

SOFIA: That's three times bigger. Where does the extra twenty-two billion come from?

MARCO: Mostly debt. Power plants and utilities are financed with huge amounts of borrowing, because their cash flows are stable and long term. But debt isn't the only thing on the bridge. Companies like AES often have outside investors who own stakes in some of their subsidiaries. That also goes on the bridge. If you only knew yesterday's formula, you'd get the number wrong.

SOFIA: So the buyers are really paying thirty-three billion.

MARCO: They're taking on the whole capital structure. Ten point seven billion goes to shareholders in cash. The rest stays in the business as obligations the new owners now carry. So when a headline says ten point seven billion, ask yourself: equity or enterprise?

SOFIA: Okay. So what's on the full bridge?

MARCO: Start with equity value. Then add debt. Add preferred shares. Add non-controlling interests, which you'll also hear called minority interests. Subtract cash. Subtract equity investments in other companies, sometimes called associates. And depending on how you define EBITDA, add lease liabilities.

SOFIA: That's a lot. Can we do it with numbers?

MARCO: Let's invent a company. Call it Cafe Group, a bigger version of our café chain. Equity value, one thousand. Debt, four hundred. Preferred shares, fifty. Non-controlling interests, thirty. Cash, one hundred. And a twenty percent stake in a coffee roaster, worth twenty.

SOFIA: So, one thousand plus four hundred plus fifty plus thirty, minus one hundred, minus twenty.

MARCO: Which is?

SOFIA: One thousand three hundred and sixty.

MARCO: Good. Now the why behind each one, because that's what the interview tests. Preferred shares first. They're a hybrid. Technically equity, but they get a fixed dividend and get paid before ordinary shareholders. To a buyer, they behave like debt: a claim you have to deal with. So you add them.

SOFIA: Okay. And minority interest. Cafe Group owns, say, eighty percent of a subsidiary, and someone else owns twenty percent. So we subtract the twenty percent, because it isn't ours.

MARCO: That's the trap, and it's the most common mistake on this whole topic. You add it. Here's why. Accounting rules say if you control a subsidiary, you consolidate one hundred percent of it. All its revenue and all its EBITDA go into Cafe Group's numbers, even though Cafe Group owns only eighty percent.

SOFIA: Oh. So the EBITDA includes the twenty percent that belongs to someone else.

MARCO: Exactly. So if you want to compare enterprise value with EBITDA, enterprise value has to include that twenty percent as well. Otherwise the numerator and the denominator don't match. Add non-controlling interests.

SOFIA: And the stake in the coffee roaster is the opposite?

MARCO: The mirror image. Cafe Group owns only twenty percent of the roaster, so it doesn't consolidate it. None of the roaster's revenue or EBITDA is in Cafe Group's numbers. But the value of that stake is sitting inside Cafe Group's share price. So you subtract it, to match EBITDA again.

SOFIA: So the rule is: whatever's in the EBITDA has to be in the enterprise value, and whatever isn't, has to come out.

MARCO: That's the single sentence that explains the whole bridge. Consistency. Numerator and denominator have to cover the same business.

SOFIA: Then the cash. We subtract all of it?

MARCO: Second trap, a smaller one. In a simple interview answer, yes. In real deals, not always. A business needs some cash just to operate: tills, payroll, a minimum balance. And some cash can be trapped, say in a foreign subsidiary where moving it home triggers tax. In real deals, buyers argue about how much cash is truly excess. In an interview, say you subtract cash, and if you want to stand out, mention that only excess cash should really come off.

SOFIA: Okay. And leases. Why are leases on the list?

MARCO: Because of an accounting change. Since twenty nineteen, under the international rules, I-F-R-S sixteen, most leases sit on the balance sheet as a liability, like debt. And the rent payments no longer hit EBITDA. They show up as depreciation and interest instead.

SOFIA: So EBITDA went up for every company that rents its shops.

MARCO: Overnight. A retailer with a lot of stores saw its EBITDA jump without anything changing in the business. So if you use that EBITDA, with rent stripped out, you have to add the lease liabilities to enterprise value, like debt. If you use EBITDA after rent, you leave leases out of enterprise value.

SOFIA: Wait. Rexel last week talked about EBITDA-a-L.

MARCO: Good memory. EBITDA after leases. Rexel reports EBITDA with the rent put back in as a cost, so its multiple should be paired with an enterprise value that excludes lease liabilities. Mix the two and your multiple is wrong. That's the third trap: inconsistent leases.

SOFIA: Okay. And the last thing. Equity value: share price times shares. Is that the full story?

MARCO: Almost. You should use diluted shares: existing shares plus options and convertible securities that would turn into shares at the current price. For a takeover, buyers count every share they'll have to pay for. We'll do dilution properly another day. For now, know the word.

SOFIA: Got it.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: walk me from equity value to enterprise value.

SOFIA: Start with equity value, add debt, preferred and minority interest, subtract cash and equity investments.

MARCO: Good list. Add the principle in one sentence: you include every claim on the business's operating assets, and remove non-operating assets, so that enterprise value matches the operating metrics, like EBITDA, that you compare it with. Then mention leases, depending on the EBITDA definition.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Why do you add non-controlling interest?

SOFIA: Because the company consolidates one hundred percent of the subsidiary's EBITDA even if it doesn't own all of it, so enterprise value has to include the part owned by others.

MARCO: Perfect. That's exactly the answer. Third: why subtract equity investments?

SOFIA: Because the company doesn't consolidate them, so their EBITDA isn't in the numbers, but their value is in the share price.

MARCO: Two clean answers. You're getting good at this.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: On Monday, Sangoma Technologies, a Canadian business communications company, agreed to be bought by B-R-C Group. Sangoma provides cloud and on-site phone and communications systems to over a hundred thousand business customers.

SOFIA: Price?

MARCO: About five dollars and twenty-three cents a share, mostly in cash with a small part in B-R-C shares. A premium of about forty-seven percent over the previous close. Equity value about one hundred and eighty million dollars. Enterprise value about two hundred and four million.

SOFIA: So the difference is twenty-four million.

MARCO: And that's your interview question. What does that difference tell you?

SOFIA: That Sangoma has about twenty-four million of net debt. More debt than cash.

MARCO: Right, or more precisely, more debt and other claims than cash and non-operating assets. You can read a company's balance sheet just from those two headline numbers. And it works for AES too: about twenty-two point seven billion between equity value and enterprise value.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: equity value eight hundred, debt three hundred, cash one hundred, non-controlling interests fifty. What's the enterprise value? Question two: enterprise value one thousand, debt four hundred, cash fifty, nothing else. What's the equity value? Question three: a company owns thirty percent of another business. Do you add or subtract that stake on the bridge? Question four: why do you add non-controlling interests? Question five: a company reports EBITDA after rent. Should its enterprise value include lease liabilities?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: eight hundred plus three hundred minus one hundred plus fifty, one thousand and fifty. Two: one thousand minus four hundred plus fifty, six hundred and fifty. Three: subtract. It's not consolidated, so its EBITDA isn't in the numbers. Four: because the parent consolidates one hundred percent of the subsidiary's EBITDA, so enterprise value must include the share owned by outsiders. Five: no. If rent is already a cost in EBITDA, leave lease liabilities out of enterprise value, or you'd count leases twice.

SOFIA: Question two caught me. I subtracted the cash.

MARCO: Going from enterprise value back to equity, everything flips. Add back cash, subtract debt. Tomorrow: multiples. E-V over EBITDA, E-V over revenue, P-E, and why a software company and a utility trade on completely different numbers.
