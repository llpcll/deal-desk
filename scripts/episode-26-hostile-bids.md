# The Deal Desk, episode 26: Hostile bids, or how Mittal Steel took Arcelor after its board said no
Mittal Steel went over the heads of Arcelor's board with an eighteen point six billion euro offer, and five months later the board recommended a much higher one. Then an American drone maker bids for an Austrian engine maker.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty-six. Yesterday's deal, Kraft and Heinz, was agreed by both boards. Today, what happens when the target's board says no and the buyer keeps going. Hostile bids. And we start with the biggest steel fight in Europe.

SOFIA: Mittal and Arcelor.

MARCO: Mittal and Arcelor. Arcelor was Europe's steel champion, based in Luxembourg, formed from French, Spanish and Luxembourg steelmakers. Mittal Steel was run by Lakshmi Mittal, whose family held ninety-seven percent of its voting rights. On the twenty-seventh of January twenty oh six, Mittal Steel made an offer straight to Arcelor's shareholders. Twenty-eight euros and twenty-one cents a share, a twenty-seven percent premium to the previous day's close. An equity value of eighteen point six billion euros. Three quarters of it paid in Mittal shares, one quarter in cash.

SOFIA: Straight to the shareholders? Not to the board?

MARCO: That's what makes it hostile. Arcelor's board rejected it. Its chief executive, Guy Dollé, compared Mittal's steel to cheap eau de cologne, and Arcelor's to fine perfume. Then came the defences. Arcelor put its Canadian business, Dofasco, into a Dutch trust, so Mittal couldn't sell it on to ThyssenKrupp as it had planned. And on the twenty-sixth of May, Arcelor agreed to merge with Severstal, the Russian steelmaker controlled by Alexei Mordashov.

SOFIA: A rescuer.

MARCO: A white knight, in the jargon. Mordashov would pay forty-four euros a share for about thirty-two percent of the combined company, and Arcelor would buy back up to a quarter of its own shares at forty-four euros. Mittal raised its offer to thirty-seven euros and seventy-four cents. On the twelfth of June the board called that inadequate and told shareholders to approve Severstal. And shareholders were furious.

SOFIA: Why? Forty-four is more than thirty-seven.

MARCO: Because Mordashov got about a third of Arcelor without bidding for the rest. And under Arcelor's rules, the Severstal deal could only be blocked if holders of half of all the shares voted against it. At the April meeting, only thirty-five percent had turned up. Shareholders holding about thirty percent demanded a new meeting. Two weeks later, on the twenty-fifth of June, the board gave in. It unanimously recommended a new Mittal offer: forty euros and forty cents a share in cash, about twenty-six billion euros. The Mittal family's stake in the combined group fell to forty-three point six percent, and Severstal was in line for a break fee of one hundred and forty million euros.

SOFIA: So the hostile bidder won, and paid a lot more.

MARCO: That's the pattern. So how does a hostile bid work? Round numbers. Delta trades at twenty euros, with one hundred million shares. A market value of two billion. Atlas, our buyer, writes privately to Delta's board, offering twenty-four euros a share. Delta's board says no.

SOFIA: Then Atlas has lost.

MARCO: Not yet. Step two, the bear hug. Atlas makes the letter public, so Delta's shareholders see the price their board turned down. Step three, Atlas goes over the board's head: a tender offer, made to shareholders directly. Say twenty-five euros. Anyone can tender their shares, and the offer only completes if Atlas ends up with more than half. Fifty million shares, plus one.

SOFIA: And the premium is twenty-five over twenty. Twenty-five percent. Though Delta's shares jumped to twenty-four when the letter came out, so really it's only about four percent.

MARCO: First trap. The premium is measured against the unaffected price, the last price before any news of a bid. Twenty euros. Twenty-four is a price that already contains the bid. And watch what happens next. Say Delta's shares trade at twenty-six, above the offer.

SOFIA: Then the market thinks the offer's too high?

MARCO: The opposite. If the shares trade above the offer, investors are betting on a higher bid, from Atlas or a rival.

SOFIA: Fine. But Delta's board can simply refuse to sell. It's their company.

MARCO: Second trap. It's the shareholders' company. The board can't stop them selling their own shares. In Britain, the Takeover Code goes further. Once a bid is coming, the board can't take action that would frustrate it, like issuing lots of new shares or selling the best asset, without shareholder approval. What the board can do is argue. Publish a defence document, show a higher valuation, promise a bigger dividend, look for a white knight.

SOFIA: And in America?

MARCO: Boards have more weapons. The best known is the poison pill: if a bidder crosses a threshold, say fifteen percent, every other shareholder can buy new shares at a deep discount, and the bidder's stake shrinks. Nobody actually wants to trigger it, so the bidder has to negotiate, or replace the board at the next shareholder meeting.

SOFIA: So hostile bids are bad news for the target's shareholders.

MARCO: Third trap. Usually the reverse. Arcelor's shareholders went from twenty-eight euros and twenty-one cents to forty euros and forty cents because their board said no first. Resistance is often a way to get a higher price. The risk is to the bidder: it bids without full access to the books, so it can overpay, and the fight costs money and time.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what is a hostile takeover, and how can a target defend itself?

SOFIA: It's when a company buys another one against its will.

MARCO: Against whose will? Strong answer: a hostile bid is one made without the support of the target's board, and usually directly to its shareholders, through a tender or exchange offer. The defences depend on the country. In America, a poison pill or a staggered board, where directors are elected in groups over several years, slows the bidder down. In Britain the board can't frustrate a bid without shareholders' approval, so it defends with arguments: a higher valuation, more cash returned, a white knight, and sometimes regulators or politicians. Arcelor used almost all of these.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: You advise a target that has received an unsolicited offer at a thirty percent premium. What do you tell the board?

SOFIA: Reject it. It undervalues the company.

MARCO: Maybe, but you haven't checked. Strong answer: the board's duty is to the shareholders, so compare the offer with what Delta is worth on its own. A D-C-F, trading comparables, precedent transactions, the football field from episode twelve. If it's below that, reject it and say why, in numbers. Then engage, because talking is how you get a higher price. And look for alternatives, so that "no" carries weight. Mittal's offer went up about forty-three percent from start to finish.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: An American buyer and an Austrian target. On Monday, the nineteenth of October, Red Cat Holdings, an American drone maker listed on Nasdaq, announced a proposed voluntary public offer for all of Steyr Motors, based in the Austrian town of Steyr. Thirty-nine euros a share, in cash. That's a premium of twenty-eight point nine percent to the closing price on Friday the sixteenth of October, and twenty-seven point three percent to the thirty-day volume-weighted average.

SOFIA: What does Steyr make?

MARCO: Engines. Small, powerful special engines, mainly for military vehicles and boats, and as auxiliary power units for main battle tanks. It has about one hundred and fifty employees, and press reports put the offer at about two hundred million euros.

SOFIA: Is this one hostile?

MARCO: No, and that's why it's today's deal. The two sides held talks earlier this year, which broke off in July. Then they resumed. This time Steyr's supervisory board chairman says the board views the deal favourably, but it will only give its formal position after reading the offer document. Under Austrian takeover rules, the target's boards have to publish a reasoned statement on any offer.

SOFIA: So the board has said yes. It's done.

MARCO: That's the trap from the start of the episode, from the other side. The board doesn't sell the company. The shareholders do. Red Cat needs acceptances for more than half the shares, fifty percent plus one. It's started with a cushion. The anchor shareholder, B and C Holding Österreich, has signed an irrevocable undertaking to tender its stake of about fifteen percent, and the chief executive, Julian Cassutti, intends to tender too.

SOFIA: So it needs another thirty-five percent from everyone else.

MARCO: Roughly, yes. The acceptance period should end on the eighteenth of December. And there are approvals: foreign investment reviews in Austria, Italy, Australia and the United Kingdom, because a defence supplier is changing hands. Closing is expected in the first half of twenty twenty-seven.

SOFIA: And the premium. Twenty-eight point nine percent. I'd say it's also fifty-five percent, because Red Cat says it's fifty-five point eight percent over the fifty-two-week low.

MARCO: Every premium is true. That's why there are several. But the low is the flattering one. The unaffected close, twenty-eight point nine percent, is the number to quote. And because the July talks were public, check that the shares weren't already carrying some bid hope by October.

SOFIA: Why secure the fifteen percent before announcing?

MARCO: That's your interview question. Strong answer: an irrevocable takes part of the threshold off the table before day one, and signals to other shareholders that a large, informed holder thinks the price is fair. It also makes a rival bid harder, because the rival starts fifteen percent behind.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: Delta's unaffected share price is twenty euros, and Atlas offers twenty-five. What is the premium? Question two: under the British Takeover Code, what can't a target board do once a bid is coming, without shareholder approval? Question three: who was Arcelor's white knight? Question four: a target's shares trade above the offer price. What is the market telling you? Question five: Red Cat needs fifty percent plus one of Steyr Motors. Roughly how much has it secured through irrevocable undertakings?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: twenty-five percent, measured against the unaffected price, not the price after the leak. Two: take action that would frustrate the bid, such as issuing new shares or selling the best asset. Three: Severstal, controlled by Alexei Mordashov. Four: investors expect a higher bid. Five: about fifteen percent, from the anchor shareholder, B and C Holding Österreich.

SOFIA: So a hostile bid isn't really against the company. It's an argument with the board, settled by the shareholders.

MARCO: And the shareholders settle it with a price. Tomorrow, episode twenty-seven: the I-P-O process, how a company sells its shares to the public for the first time, with Ferrari in twenty fifteen and Arm in twenty twenty-three.
