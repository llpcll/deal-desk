# The Deal Desk, episode 20: Valuing banks and insurers, or the bank that sold for a fifteenth of its book
In March twenty twenty-three, U-B-S paid three billion francs for a bank whose accounts showed forty-five billion of equity. Nobody believed the accounts.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode twenty. So far we've valued companies by their cash flows, split between lenders and shareholders. Today, the companies where that split stops making sense: banks and insurers. And we start on a Sunday evening in Switzerland.

SOFIA: A deal on a Sunday. That's rarely good news.

MARCO: It wasn't. On the nineteenth of March twenty twenty-three, the Swiss government announced that U-B-S would take over its old rival, Credit Suisse, a bank founded in eighteen fifty-six. In the week before, Credit Suisse had borrowed fifty-four billion dollars from the Swiss central bank, and the money was still leaving.

SOFIA: What did U-B-S pay?

MARCO: Three billion Swiss francs, in U-B-S shares. One U-B-S share for every twenty-two point four eight Credit Suisse shares, about seventy-six centimes each. On the Friday, Credit Suisse had closed at one franc eighty-six.

SOFIA: Less than half of Friday's price. Harsh.

MARCO: Now the number that matters for today. Credit Suisse's last annual accounts, for twenty twenty-two, showed shareholders' equity of about forty-five billion francs. That's the book value. U-B-S paid three.

SOFIA: Three billion for forty-five? That's a fifteenth. U-B-S got the bargain of the century.

MARCO: The accounts agreed with you. When U-B-S reported its second quarter, in August, it booked negative goodwill of twenty-eight point nine billion dollars: a gain, because it had paid so far below the value of what it bought. But look at why the price was so low. In twenty twenty-two Credit Suisse lost about seven point three billion francs. To get the deal done, the Swiss state guaranteed up to nine billion francs of losses, and sixteen billion francs of A-T-one bonds, a kind of debt designed to absorb losses, were written down to zero.

SOFIA: So the book said forty-five, and nobody trusted the book.

MARCO: That's today's episode. For a bank, the balance sheet is the business. So the questions are different: what is the book really worth, and can the bank earn a decent return on it?

SOFIA: Why wouldn't it work? Take E-V to EBITDA, like we did for Clarion.

MARCO: Try it. Take a simple bank, call it Safe Bank. It takes in deposits, lends them out, and earns the gap between the two rates. What's its debt?

SOFIA: The deposits. So enterprise value is the shares plus all the deposits.

MARCO: First trap. For Cafe Group, debt is how the business is funded. For a bank, deposits are the raw material, the way coffee beans are for Cafe Group. And the interest it pays isn't a financing cost, it's its main cost of doing business. Strip it out to get EBITDA, and you've removed half the business. So for banks, enterprise value and EBITDA don't tell you anything useful. You value the equity directly.

SOFIA: Then unlevered free cash flow and the weighted average cost of capital are out too.

MARCO: Out, for the same reason. Yesterday I said levered thinking earns its place at banks. Here's why. Safe Bank has book equity of one thousand. Millions of euros, if you like. It earns net income of one hundred and twenty, so its return on equity, R-O-E, is twelve percent. Shareholders want ten percent: that's the cost of equity. And the bank grows two percent a year.

SOFIA: So the free cash flow is one hundred and twenty. A bank doesn't build factories, so there's no capex to take off.

MARCO: Second trap. A bank has its own version of capex: capital. Regulators make it hold equity against every loan. To grow its loans by two percent, Safe Bank must grow its equity by two percent. That's twenty, kept back out of the one hundred and twenty. Only the other one hundred can go to shareholders. That hundred is the bank's free cash flow to equity.

SOFIA: And I discount it at the cost of equity. The matching rule from yesterday.

MARCO: Exactly. One hundred, divided by ten percent minus two percent. One hundred over eight percent: one thousand two hundred and fifty. That's a dividend discount model, the banking world's D-C-F.

SOFIA: One thousand two hundred and fifty, on a book of one thousand. So one point two five times book.

MARCO: Price to book, the first multiple anyone checks for a bank. And there's a shortcut. Price to book equals return on equity minus growth, over cost of equity minus growth. Twelve minus two is ten. Ten minus two is eight. Ten over eight: one point two five.

SOFIA: Same answer.

MARCO: Same model, written differently. Now cut Safe Bank's return to eight percent. Net income eighty. It still keeps back twenty to grow, so dividends are sixty. Sixty over eight percent: seven hundred and fifty. Point seven five times book.

SOFIA: So when a bank trades below book, it's cheap. Buy it.

MARCO: Third trap. A bank below book is usually telling you it earns less than its cost of equity. Eight percent earned, ten percent required, so each euro of book is worth seventy-five cents. And if the market suspects the book itself is overstated, with bad loans not yet written off, the discount gets deeper. Credit Suisse was losing money. A fifteenth of book wasn't a bargain price. It was the price of not knowing what the book was worth.

SOFIA: And price to earnings? Does that still work?

MARCO: It works. Safe Bank at one thousand two hundred and fifty, on one hundred and twenty of earnings: about ten times. But a bank's earnings swing with bad loans, so price to book anchors the valuation. Many bankers prefer price to tangible book: strip out goodwill and other intangibles, because regulators don't count them as capital. Credit Suisse's tangible equity was about forty-two billion francs.

SOFIA: And insurers? Same idea?

MARCO: Same family. An insurer collects premiums today, pays claims later, and invests the money in between. That pool of money, like a bank's deposits, is part of the business, not its funding. So again you value the equity: price to book, price to earnings, and dividends limited by capital. In Europe the capital rules are called Solvency Two, and the number you'll hear is the solvency ratio: the capital an insurer holds, divided by the capital it's required to hold. For life insurers, whose contracts run for decades, there's also embedded value: the book plus today's value of the profits on policies already sold.

SOFIA: So the higher the solvency ratio, the better the insurer.

MARCO: Fourth trap. Safer, yes. Better, not past a point. Capital sitting idle earns little, which drags down the return on equity, and from our formula, the price to book. Investors want enough capital, then the rest paid out.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: why can't you use enterprise value to value a bank?

SOFIA: Because banks have so much debt that the number gets enormous.

MARCO: The size isn't the problem. The strong answer: for a bank, debt and deposits are the raw material of the business, and interest is an operating cost, not a financing choice. So you can't separate the business from its funding, which is the whole point of enterprise value. Value the equity directly, with price to book, price to tangible book, price to earnings, and a dividend discount model where dividends are limited by regulatory capital.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: A bank earns a return on equity of fifteen percent. Its cost of equity is ten percent and it grows five percent a year. What price to book should it trade at?

SOFIA: Fifteen over ten. One point five times.

MARCO: Close, but you've forgotten growth. Take it off both. Fifteen minus five is ten, ten minus five is five. Ten over five: two times book. Growth pushes the multiple up when the return beats the cost of equity.

SOFIA: Third?

MARCO: A bank trades at half its book value. Is it cheap?

SOFIA: Yes. You're buying a euro of equity for fifty cents.

MARCO: Only if the euro is real and earns its keep. The strong answer: a price to book of point five usually means the market expects the bank to earn well below its cost of equity, or doubts the book, because of bad loans or assets that may need writing down. It's cheap only if you think returns will recover, or the book is better than the market believes. Credit Suisse is the warning.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A French bank and a Spanish one. On Tuesday, the sixth of October, B-P-C-E, the second-largest banking group in France, owned by its regional savings and cooperative banks, announced it had bought about seven percent of Banco Sabadell, Spain's fourth-largest bank.

SOFIA: Seven percent. That's not a takeover.

MARCO: And both sides were clear about that. B-P-C-E says it doesn't intend to go above nine point nine percent. It wants a seat on Sabadell's board, subject to approvals, and the two banks will explore working together in corporate and investment banking and consumer credit, with talks expected to conclude in early twenty twenty-seven. Neither bank disclosed the price paid. At market prices, Reuters put the stake at about one point two billion euros.

SOFIA: So the whole of Sabadell is worth about seventeen billion euros.

MARCO: Roughly, yes. And it's a big balance sheet: at the end of twenty twenty-five, about one hundred and seventy-nine billion euros of deposits and one hundred and sixty-three billion of loans.

SOFIA: Why does a friendly seven percent matter?

MARCO: History. Last year Sabadell fought off a hostile bid from B-B-V-A, and analysts quoted by Reuters saw a friendly long-term shareholder as protection against the next one. B-P-C-E also knows the region: last year it bought Novo Banco, in Portugal, for six point four billion euros. So here's your interview question. You're advising B-P-C-E. How do you judge whether one point two billion euros is a fair price for seven percent of Sabadell?

SOFIA: I'd compare Sabadell's E-V to EBITDA with other Spanish banks.

MARCO: That's the first trap again. Use price to tangible book, and compare it with Spanish and European peers, alongside each bank's return on tangible equity, because the return explains the multiple. A bank earning more should trade at more times book. Then check it with a dividend discount model, with payouts limited by the capital Sabadell must hold. And remember it's a minority stake bought at market prices, so there's no control premium in that one point two billion.

SOFIA: So for a bank, you start from the book, then ask what the book can earn.

MARCO: Nicely put. Quiz. For you listening. Question one: why don't bankers value a bank with E-V to EBITDA? Question two: a bank earns a return on equity of twelve percent, its cost of equity is ten percent, and it grows two percent a year. What price to book should it trade at? Question three: Safe Bank earns one hundred and twenty on one thousand of equity and grows two percent a year. How much can it pay out in dividends? Question four: a bank trades at point six times book. What's the most likely reason? Question five: a seven percent stake in Sabadell is worth about one point two billion euros. Roughly what is the whole bank worth?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: because deposits and interest are part of a bank's business, not its funding, so you value the equity directly. Two: one point two five times book. Twelve minus two, over ten minus two. Three: one hundred. It keeps twenty back to grow its capital by two percent. Four: the market expects it to earn less than its cost of equity, or doubts its book value. Five: about seventeen billion euros. One point two billion divided by seven percent.

SOFIA: So we've now valued a company every way you can, from the cash flows to the book.

MARCO: Which makes it a good moment to check it's stuck. Tomorrow, episode twenty-one: the week three review. The D-C-F from end to end, from free cash flow to the share price, tested the way an interviewer would. No notes.
