# The Deal Desk, episode 15: Unlevered free cash flow, or who pays for the capex
Warren Buffett once showed the same company's year with two very different profits. He trusted neither. He trusted the cash.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode fifteen. Two days ago we built a D-C-F with a café that simply handed over a hundred of cash a year. Real companies don't hand you that number. You have to build it. Today: unlevered free cash flow. And we start in Omaha.

SOFIA: Warren Buffett.

MARCO: Right after the end of nineteen eighty-five, Berkshire Hathaway bought Scott Fetzer, a group of seventeen businesses including World Book encyclopaedias and Kirby vacuum cleaners. About three hundred and twenty million dollars, with another ninety million dollars of debt left in place.

SOFIA: Where's the twist?

MARCO: The accounting. Buffett paid far more than the value on Scott Fetzer's books, so Berkshire had to restate its assets at a higher value and depreciate and amortise them for years. In his letter to shareholders, he showed nineteen eighty-six two ways. The old Scott Fetzer, as if the deal never happened, earned about forty point two million dollars of net income. The new Scott Fetzer, inside Berkshire, earned about twenty-eight point six million.

SOFIA: Same factories, same customers, and nearly a third of the profit disappears?

MARCO: Only on paper. Not a dollar of cash changed. So Buffett asked what an owner can actually take out of a business. He called it owner earnings: reported earnings, plus depreciation and amortisation, minus the capital spending the business needs to stay competitive. On that measure, he said, the two companies were identical. And he had no patience for people who add back depreciation and forget the spending. Years later he wrote that references to EBITDA made him shudder. Does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?

SOFIA: So profit is an opinion.

MARCO: Profit is accounting. Cash is what you can spend. And a D-C-F discounts cash. Let's build it. Remember Cafe Group from yesterday? EBITDA of one hundred and thirty.

SOFIA: Then that's the cash. One hundred and thirty.

MARCO: First trap, the tooth fairy trap. EBITDA ignores three things that cost real money: tax, investment in equipment, and cash tied up in running the business. We'll take them one at a time. Depreciation and amortisation are thirty, so EBIT, operating profit, is one hundred.

SOFIA: Then I take off the interest on Cafe Group's debt, say twenty, and tax the rest.

MARCO: Second trap. Unlevered means we pretend the company has no debt. We want the cash available to everyone who funded the business, lenders and shareholders together. So no interest. Tax at twenty-five percent on the full one hundred of EBIT: twenty-five of tax, seventy-five left.

SOFIA: But the company really does pay interest. And it really saves tax on it.

MARCO: Good instinct, and that saving isn't lost. It goes into the discount rate, the weighted average cost of capital, which is episode sixteen. Count it in the cash flow too and you count it twice. Next: add back the thirty of depreciation and amortisation. It reduced profit, but no cash left the building. One hundred and five.

SOFIA: So depreciation doesn't matter at all?

MARCO: It matters once, through tax. It lowers taxable profit, so the company pays less tax. Now the spending. Capital expenditure, new ovens, new cafés, refits: forty. Down to sixty-five.

SOFIA: And then working capital. That's week one, episode six. Cafe Group's working capital went up by fifteen, so I add fifteen.

MARCO: Third trap, and the most common one in interviews. When working capital goes up, cash goes down. More stock in the stores, or customers paying later, means money sitting in the business instead of your pocket. Subtract fifteen. Unlevered free cash flow: fifty.

SOFIA: Fifty. From an EBITDA of one hundred and thirty.

MARCO: Less than forty percent of it. That's the gap Buffett was warning about. And look where every line came from. EBIT from the income statement. Depreciation, capex and the change in working capital from the cash flow statement. The balance sheet sits behind all of it. Week one's three statements, in one calculation.

SOFIA: So the formula is EBIT times one minus the tax rate, plus depreciation and amortisation, minus capex, minus the increase in working capital.

MARCO: Exactly. Now a classic. Cafe Group's depreciation rises by ten, and nothing else changes. What happens to free cash flow?

SOFIA: It falls by ten. Higher costs.

MARCO: Fourth trap. EBIT falls by ten, so tax falls by two and a half. Then you add back all ten of the depreciation. Free cash flow goes up by two and a half: the tax saving. Fifty-two and a half.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: how do you get from EBITDA to unlevered free cash flow?

SOFIA: EBITDA minus capex minus taxes.

MARCO: Close, but which taxes? Say it properly. Take EBITDA, subtract depreciation and amortisation to get EBIT. Tax EBIT at the company's rate, ignoring interest. Add back depreciation and amortisation and any other non-cash charges. Subtract capital expenditure and the increase in net working capital. That's cash available to all capital providers.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Why do we use unlevered cash flow, and not the cash left after paying the banks?

SOFIA: Because interest is usually small?

MARCO: No. Because it's a matching rule. Unlevered free cash flow belongs to lenders and shareholders together, so you discount it at the weighted average cost of capital and you get enterprise value. Cash after interest belongs only to shareholders; that's levered free cash flow, discounted at the cost of equity, and it gives equity value. Episode nineteen. Mix the two and the answer is wrong.

SOFIA: Third?

MARCO: A company's EBITDA is growing every year, but its free cash flow is falling. How?

SOFIA: The tax rate went up?

MARCO: Possible, but think bigger. Usually heavy investment: capex running well ahead of depreciation to build capacity. Or working capital swallowing cash: a growing company sells more on credit and holds more stock. A fast-growing business can be very profitable and short of cash at the same time.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: From Milan, and it's a fight about cash flow forecasts. Recordati, the Italian drugmaker. This year the private equity firm C-V-C, with the Belgian investment company Groupe Bruxelles Lambert, offered to buy the shares it doesn't already own. C-V-C holds about forty-seven percent. The offer was fifty-one euros twenty-nine a share.

SOFIA: And what happened this week?

MARCO: On Tuesday, the sixth of October, they raised it to fifty-three euros, and called it their best and final offer. Add the seventy-one cents of dividend already paid to shareholders and the total is fifty-three euros seventy-one. That's sixteen point six percent above the closing price on the twenty-fifth of March, the last trading day before the bid was announced.

SOFIA: Sixteen percent is low, isn't it? You said twenty to forty is normal.

MARCO: It's low. And that's why they had to raise it. Back in July, Recordati's board split, six votes to four. The four independent directors had their own adviser, Rothschild. The board's adviser was Lazard. Both built a D-C-F. Lazard's range was roughly forty-five to fifty-four euros a share. Rothschild's was roughly fifty-two to sixty-one.

SOFIA: Dell all over again. Same company, same method, two answers.

MARCO: Same company, same method, different forecasts of exactly what we built today. How fast sales grow, how much the business has to spend to keep growing, how much cash it ties up. Then a discount rate on top. And outside the boardroom, the activist fund Palliser Capital says the price should be at least sixty euros. The average analyst target, according to Il Sole Twenty-Four Ore, is about sixty.

SOFIA: So why should anyone accept fifty-three?

MARCO: That's your interview question. What would you tell a shareholder?

SOFIA: Reject it. Rothschild says it's worth up to sixty-one.

MARCO: Too quick. The strong answer puts both ranges on the football field. Fifty-three is near the top of Lazard's range and near the bottom of Rothschild's, so the question is whose cash flow forecasts you believe. Then the market check: the bidders point to falling valuations across European healthcare and specialty pharma. And then tactics. As of the fifth of October, shares tendered, including C-V-C's own stake, came to about fifty percent of the company. The offer now runs until the twenty-third of October.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: EBIT is two hundred, the tax rate is twenty-five percent, depreciation is forty, capex is sixty and working capital rises by twenty. What's unlevered free cash flow? Question two: working capital goes up by ten. Does free cash flow go up or down? Question three: why don't you subtract interest when you calculate unlevered free cash flow? Question four: depreciation rises by twenty, with a tax rate of twenty-five percent. What happens to unlevered free cash flow? Question five: Lazard's D-C-F range for Recordati was roughly forty-five to fifty-four euros, and Rothschild's roughly fifty-two to sixty-one. Where does the fifty-three euro offer sit?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: one hundred and ten. Two hundred minus fifty of tax is one hundred and fifty, plus forty, minus sixty, minus twenty. Two: down. Cash is tied up in the business. Three: because unlevered cash flow belongs to lenders and shareholders together. The cost of debt and its tax saving go into the weighted average cost of capital instead. Four: it goes up by five, the tax saving. Twenty times twenty-five percent. Five: near the top of Lazard's range and near the bottom of Rothschild's.

SOFIA: So the tooth fairy doesn't pay for the ovens.

MARCO: And she doesn't set the discount rate either. Tomorrow, episode sixteen: the weighted average cost of capital, the capital asset pricing model and beta. And what happened to valuations in twenty twenty-two, when interest rates jumped and every discount rate moved with them.
