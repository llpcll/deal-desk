# The Deal Desk, episode 42: Season review and mock superday, or what Bayer really bought with Monsanto
In twenty eighteen Bayer completed its sixty-three billion dollar purchase of Monsanto, and within a year most votes at its annual meeting went against its management. Six weeks in three mock interviews. Then Mare Group buys sixty percent of an Italian circuit-board maker for two point four million euros.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode forty-two, the last of season one. Six weeks: the statements, value, the D-C-F, mergers and acquisitions, private equity, and this past week's interview skills. Today Sofia sits a mock superday, the final round, where you meet several interviewers one after another. Three interviews, no notes. If you're listening, answer before she does.

SOFIA: And the first thing they'll say is: tell me about a deal.

MARCO: So here's one, and you'll tell it back to me. On the fourteenth of September twenty sixteen, Bayer, the German drugs and crop chemicals group, agreed to buy Monsanto, the American seed and weedkiller company. One hundred and twenty-eight dollars a share in cash, a forty-four percent premium to the price before the talks became public, and about sixty-six billion dollars including debt. It closed on the seventh of June twenty eighteen, at about sixty-three billion.

SOFIA: And then?

MARCO: Nine weeks later, on the tenth of August, a California jury awarded two hundred and eighty-nine million dollars to a former school groundskeeper who said Monsanto's weedkiller, Roundup, had given him terminal cancer. On appeal that was cut to twenty point five million. But on the next trading day Bayer's shares fell about ten percent by midday, because investors weren't only pricing one case. They were pricing the cases behind it. In April twenty nineteen, fifty-five point five percent of the votes at Bayer's annual meeting went against approving its management board. It wasn't binding, but Fortune called it unprecedented, and reckoned the lawsuits had cut about two-fifths off Bayer's value. And it isn't over. In February this year Monsanto proposed a settlement of seven point two five billion dollars for current and future claims, and at the end of October a Missouri judge still hadn't approved it.

SOFIA: It was a terrible deal. Bayer overpaid by forty-four percent for a company with a lawsuit problem.

MARCO: First trap, from episode thirty-six. There's no headline, and the view doesn't say for whom. For Monsanto's shareholders it was excellent: a forty-four percent premium, in cash, and the risk became someone else's. And a premium isn't an overpayment on its own, because you have to compare it with what the buyer gets. Good for whom, at this price, because of what. For Bayer: buy a company's shares and you buy its liabilities too, and the price depended on a risk it was hard to size.

MARCO: Interview one, the accounts and value. Weeks one and two. Depreciation goes up by ten, tax at twenty-five percent. Go.

SOFIA: Operating profit down ten, tax down two point five, net income down seven point five. Add back the ten, so cash is up two point five. Assets and retained earnings both down seven point five. It balances.

MARCO: Interview speed. Now Harbour. Twenty euros a share, fifty million shares, five hundred million euros of net debt, one hundred and fifty million of EBITDA. Equity value, enterprise value, multiple.

SOFIA: One billion of equity, one point five billion of enterprise value, ten times EBITDA.

MARCO: Harbour now pays out one hundred million as a cash dividend. What happens to enterprise value?

SOFIA: It falls by one hundred. There's less money in the company.

MARCO: Second trap. Equity value falls by one hundred, because the cash has left. Net debt rises by one hundred, for the same reason. Enterprise value doesn't move, because the business hasn't changed. It's the price of the operations, however they're funded.

MARCO: Interview two, the D-C-F and mergers and acquisitions. Weeks three and four. A business will make free cash flow of one hundred next year, growing at two percent forever. The discount rate is ten percent. What's it worth today?

SOFIA: One hundred over ten percent. One thousand.

MARCO: Third trap. You left out the growth. Cash flow over the discount rate minus growth: one hundred over eight percent, one thousand two hundred and fifty. Next. Northgate has earnings of one hundred million euros and one hundred million shares at twenty euros, so twenty times earnings. It pays six hundred million in new shares for a company earning fifty million. Does that add to earnings per share, or reduce them?

SOFIA: Reduce them. It's issuing new shares, so the earnings are split among more people.

MARCO: Fourth trap. Thirty million new shares, yes, but fifty million of earnings arrives with them. One hundred and fifty over one hundred and thirty: earnings per share go from one euro to about one euro fifteen. Accretive. The check: Northgate trades at twenty times and buys at twelve. In an all-share deal, buying below your own multiple adds to earnings per share.

MARCO: Interview three, private equity, then you. Weeks five and six. A fund buys at eight times EBITDA of one hundred: five hundred of debt, three hundred of equity. Five years later, EBITDA is one hundred and fifty, the multiple is the same, and debt is down to three hundred. The return, out loud.

SOFIA: Eight times one hundred and fifty: one thousand two hundred. Minus three hundred of debt: nine hundred. Three times my money, about twenty-five percent a year. And the six hundred of gain is four hundred from growth and two hundred from paying down debt.

MARCO: Good. Tripling in five years is about twenty-five percent a year, and sixty is what simple interest would tell you. Now the bad case. The debt is three hundred of senior loans and two hundred of bonds, and the business is worth four hundred. Who gets what?

SOFIA: Seniors three hundred, bonds one hundred, fifty cents on the euro. The shareholders get nothing.

MARCO: Right, and the bonds are where the value runs out, so they're the fulcrum. Last question of the day. Do you have any questions for me?

SOFIA: No, thank you. I think you've covered everything.

MARCO: Fifth trap, and the last thing they'll remember. It sounds as if you've stopped being curious. Ask something you couldn't find on the website, about a deal they worked on or a person you spoke to. Same rule as why this bank.

SOFIA: So where did I slip?

MARCO: Five places, one habit: you read one side of the number. The buyer's side of the premium, the equity side of the dividend, the new shares without the earnings. And the numerical test comes before all of this: read the question first, ask percent of what, check the direction, and keep moving.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First, from the markets person on the panel: would you buy Bayer's shares?

SOFIA: Yes. It's a great company, and in June the American Supreme Court ruled for Monsanto, seven to two.

MARCO: That ruling is in the share price already. Strong answer: what's priced in, where you differ, the catalyst, and the risk against the reward. The ruling blocks claims about the missing cancer warning, but not every kind of claim. The catalyst is the Missouri judge's decision on the settlement.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: You give a wrong answer, and the interviewer tells you so. What do you do?

SOFIA: Apologise, and ask to move on.

MARCO: Moving on is the one thing you can't do. Strong answer: ask for a moment, find the mistake out loud, give the corrected number, and say what it means. That's the follow-up test from episode thirty-nine.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A small one you can do in your head, and it's Italian. On Saturday the thirty-first of October, Mare Group, an engineering company listed on Euronext Growth Milan, signed a binding agreement to buy sixty percent of Assel, of Aprilia, south of Rome. Since nineteen ninety-one Assel has made electronic circuit boards and repaired equipment for aerospace and defence, and Leonardo is among its customers. Mare announced it on Monday. Question one, who and why.

SOFIA: A buyer bringing a supplier in-house.

MARCO: Alantra's analysts count it as Mare's fifth aerospace and defence purchase in about eighteen months. Question two. Sixty percent costs two point four million euros.

SOFIA: So Assel is worth two point four million.

MARCO: Sixty percent of it is. All the equity is worth four million, and the enterprise value is two and a half million.

SOFIA: Enterprise value below equity value? That's the wrong way round.

MARCO: Sixth trap, and it's Harbour's dividend in reverse. Assel has net cash, one and a half million euros at the end of September. Four minus one and a half: two and a half. The buyer pays for the cash, and then owns it.

SOFIA: Then the multiple. Two and a half million over expected EBITDA of six hundred thousand, about four point two times. Alantra says Mare itself trades at seven point four. So it's cheap.

MARCO: Seventh trap. Times which EBITDA? Six hundred thousand is the forecast for twenty twenty-six. In twenty twenty-five Assel made about two hundred thousand, on production value of four and a half million. On that it's twelve and a half times. The price is low only if EBITDA triples in a year, while production value rises to about five million.

SOFIA: Question three, the payment.

MARCO: Ninety-five percent in cash, about two point three million. Five percent in Mare's own shares, locked up for twenty-four months. For the other forty percent, Mare has an option to buy and the sellers have an option to sell, in the six months after the third anniversary of closing, at six times average adjusted EBITDA, with an estimated minimum of one point six million. Question four, does it work? If the six hundred thousand arrives. The Flamini family, who run Assel, have committed to stay for three years, and the option rewards them at six times if EBITDA grows. Question five, what could stop it? Italy's golden power rules, which let the state review deals in strategic sectors. Closing is expected by the thirty-first of December.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: Harbour pays a one hundred million euro cash dividend. What happens to its enterprise value? Question two: free cash flow of one hundred next year, growing at two percent forever, with a discount rate of ten percent. What's it worth? Question three: a fund turns three hundred of equity into nine hundred in five years. Roughly what I-R-R? Question four: why is Assel's enterprise value below its equity value? Question five: Assel's enterprise value is two and a half million euros. What's the multiple on twenty twenty-five EBITDA of two hundred thousand?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: it doesn't change. Equity value falls by one hundred and net debt rises by one hundred. Two: one thousand two hundred and fifty, one hundred over eight percent. One thousand leaves out the growth. Three: about twenty-five percent, three times in five years. Sixty is simple interest. Four: net cash. Four million of equity minus one and a half million of net cash is two and a half million. Five: twelve and a half times, against about four point two on the twenty twenty-six forecast.

SOFIA: Every number has another number behind it. Six weeks ago I'd have stopped at the first one.
