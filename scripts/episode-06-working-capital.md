# The Deal Desk, episode 06: Working capital, and how Amazon gets its suppliers to fund it
Amazon's customers pay it about two months before Amazon pays its suppliers. That gap is worth billions.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode six. Today, working capital. It sounds like the dullest topic in finance. It's actually the reason some companies survive recessions and others don't. Start with Amazon.

SOFIA: Amazon's hardly a survival story.

MARCO: It was, once. Go back to two thousand and two thousand and one. The dot-com bubble bursts. Hundreds of internet companies die. Pets dot com, which had raised a fortune and bought a Super Bowl advert, shut down in November two thousand. Amazon's share price collapsed too. And Amazon was losing money.

SOFIA: So why didn't it die?

MARCO: Partly because of how its cash moves. When you buy something on Amazon, you pay by card the moment you order. Amazon has your cash within a day or two. But Amazon pays the company that supplied that product weeks later. So every sale puts cash in Amazon's bank before Amazon has to pay for the product.

SOFIA: So its suppliers are funding it.

MARCO: Effectively, yes. For free. And the faster Amazon grows, the more cash that gap produces. It's the opposite of most businesses, where growth sucks cash in. Harvard Business School uses it as the textbook case. In the early years, Amazon held very little inventory, collected from customers almost immediately, and paid suppliers after about a month.

SOFIA: And today?

MARCO: The gap has got wider. On the figures for twenty twenty-five from one analysis of Amazon's accounts, Amazon holds inventory for about thirty-nine days, collects from customers in about twenty-one days, and pays suppliers after about a hundred and twenty-five days.

SOFIA: A hundred and twenty-five days?

MARCO: Roughly four months. Put those together and Amazon gets its cash about sixty-five days before it has to pay it out. Across a business of Amazon's size, that's tens of billions of dollars of interest-free funding. That's what we're learning today.

SOFIA: Okay. So what exactly is working capital?

MARCO: The textbook definition is current assets minus current liabilities. Current means due or usable within a year. But for valuation and deals, bankers usually mean operating working capital: the short-term items tied to running the business. Receivables, plus inventory, minus payables. We leave out cash and debt, because those are about financing, not operations.

SOFIA: The café again?

MARCO: The café. Receivables of thirty, the catering clients who haven't paid. Inventory of fifty, beans and pastries. Payables of forty, what we owe suppliers. Thirty plus fifty minus forty: net working capital of forty. That's forty thousand euros of the business's money tied up in running it day to day.

SOFIA: So more working capital is better. More of a cushion.

MARCO: That's the first trap, and it depends which way you look at it. For a lender asking can you pay your bills, having more current assets than current liabilities is comforting. But for cash flow and valuation, working capital is cash you can't use. It's stuck on the shelf or with your customers. If working capital goes up, cash goes down. That's why, on the cash flow statement, an increase in working capital is a minus.

SOFIA: Right, we did that on Saturday. Receivables up, cash down.

MARCO: Exactly. Now, the key measure. The cash conversion cycle. How many days between paying for your inventory and getting cash from the customer. Three pieces. Days inventory outstanding: how long stock sits before it's sold. Days sales outstanding: how long customers take to pay. Days payables outstanding: how long you take to pay suppliers.

SOFIA: And the cycle is inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days.

MARCO: Right. Say the café holds stock for ten days, catering clients pay in thirty, and we pay suppliers in twenty. Ten plus thirty minus twenty: twenty days. For twenty days, every euro of sales is money we've spent but not yet got back.

SOFIA: And Amazon is thirty-nine plus twenty-one minus a hundred and twenty-five.

MARCO: Minus sixty-five days. Negative. It gets paid first.

SOFIA: Okay but negative working capital usually means a company is in trouble, doesn't it? It can't cover its short-term liabilities.

MARCO: Second trap. It can mean trouble, if the company simply can't pay its suppliers on time. But in some business models, it's a sign of strength. Supermarkets, fast food chains, subscription software companies that bill a year upfront, airlines selling tickets months before you fly. They all collect before they pay. The question you ask is why it's negative. Is it bargaining power over suppliers and customers who pay upfront? Or is it a company that's behind on its bills?

SOFIA: Okay. And for a normal company, if it grows fast, it makes more money, so it has more cash.

MARCO: Third trap, and it kills real companies. A normal business has positive working capital. If sales double, receivables and inventory roughly double too. That's more cash tied up. A company can grow fast, be profitable on paper, and still run out of cash, because growth ate it. Bankers call it growing broke.

SOFIA: So for Amazon, growth creates cash, and for a normal company, growth uses cash.

MARCO: That's the sentence. Remember it.

SOFIA: Then why doesn't every company just pay suppliers later? Isn't that free money?

MARCO: Good question. It isn't free. Suppliers aren't stupid. If you pay late, they raise prices, cut you off, or ask for cash upfront. Amazon can do it because it's the biggest customer many suppliers have. A small company that stretches payables usually just damages its relationships. And some companies go further and use supply chain finance, where a bank pays the supplier early and the company pays the bank later. It can hide how much the company really owes. That's a warning sign interviewers like to hear you know about.

SOFIA: Okay. Why does working capital matter so much in deals?

MARCO: Two reasons. One, in a D-C-F, free cash flow is cash from operations minus capex, and changes in working capital are part of cash from operations. Get working capital wrong and the valuation is wrong. Two, in the sale agreement itself. When a company is sold, the buyer and seller agree a normal level of working capital, a target or peg. If the seller delivers less than that on the closing date, the price goes down.

SOFIA: Why? What would the seller do?

MARCO: Imagine you're selling the café, and the price includes the cash in the business. Just before closing, you chase every customer to pay early, stop buying stock, and pay suppliers late. Cash goes up. You walk away with more money, and the buyer gets a café with empty shelves and angry suppliers. The peg stops that.

SOFIA: Clever. And a bit dodgy.

MARCO: That's why the lawyers and the due diligence team spend weeks on it. Right, what they'll ask you. First: what is working capital, and why does it matter?

SOFIA: It's current assets minus current liabilities, and it shows if a company can pay its short-term bills.

MARCO: Correct, and add the finance angle. The strong answer: working capital is current assets minus current liabilities; in practice, bankers focus on operating working capital, meaning receivables plus inventory minus payables. It matters because changes in it affect cash flow. An increase uses cash, a decrease releases cash. And that feeds directly into free cash flow and valuation.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Can a company have negative working capital and be healthy?

SOFIA: Yes, like Amazon, because customers pay before suppliers are paid.

MARCO: Good, and give one more example, plus the caveat. Yes: companies with strong bargaining power or upfront payments, like Amazon, supermarkets or subscription businesses. But negative working capital can also mean a company is struggling to pay suppliers, so you'd check why.

SOFIA: Third?

MARCO: A company grows revenue fifty percent in a year, it's profitable, but its cash balance falls. How?

SOFIA: Because it had to buy more inventory, and customers owe it more.

MARCO: Exactly. And say it in one phrase at the end: growth tied up cash in working capital. Plus possibly capex to support the growth.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: This one fits today perfectly. On Friday, Rexel, the French electrical distributor, agreed to buy G-C-G, an American distributor of wire, cable and power equipment for infrastructure, from Audax Private Equity. About one point four billion dollars of enterprise value.

SOFIA: What's special about G-C-G?

MARCO: Over sixty percent of its revenue comes from growth areas like data centres, utilities and grid upgrades. Everyone building data centres for A-I needs a lot of cable. Revenue this year is expected above one point one billion dollars. Rexel says it's paying under eight times EBITDA once you include expected synergies.

SOFIA: How is Rexel paying?

MARCO: A mix. Cash it already has, about eight hundred million euros of new debt, and up to five hundred million euros of new shares, sold quickly to investors through what's called an accelerated bookbuild.

SOFIA: And the link to working capital?

MARCO: Distributors are working capital businesses. They buy cable in bulk, hold it in warehouses, and give contractors time to pay. So they sit on large inventories and large receivables. And that's your interview question. Why would a buyer of a distributor care so much about working capital?

SOFIA: Because a lot of the company's money is stuck in stock and receivables?

MARCO: Right. And more precisely: because growth at a distributor eats cash. If G-C-G grows with the data centre boom, it needs more inventory and more receivables to support it. So the buyer has to model how much cash that growth will absorb. And in a deal like this, the working capital peg at closing matters a lot, because the numbers are big enough to move the real price.

SOFIA: Okay. Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: receivables are fifty, inventory seventy, payables forty. What is operating working capital? Question two: inventory days are thirty, receivable days twenty, payable days sixty. What's the cash conversion cycle? Question three: inventory goes up by fifteen. What happens to cash? Question four: payables go up by ten. What happens to cash? Question five: why can a fast-growing, profitable company run out of cash?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: fifty plus seventy minus forty, so eighty. Two: thirty plus twenty minus sixty, so minus ten days. The company gets paid ten days before it pays suppliers. Three: cash down fifteen. Four: cash up ten, because you've held on to the money longer. Five: because growth ties up cash in receivables and inventory before the profit arrives as cash. Growing broke.

SOFIA: That's a good phrase.

MARCO: It'll impress an interviewer if you can explain it. Tomorrow is a review episode. I'll quiz you on the whole of week one, and you won't have your notes.
