# The Deal Desk, episode 11: Trading comparables, and is Ferrari a car company?
Ferrari trades at more than twice the multiple of a typical car maker. Whether that's right depends on one question: who are its peers?

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode eleven. Yesterday, multiples. Today, the most common way bankers use them: trading comparables, or comps. And the best way in is a question that sounds silly. Is Ferrari a car company?

SOFIA: It makes cars. In Maranello. Yes?

MARCO: Look at the numbers first. In twenty twenty-five, Ferrari shipped thirteen thousand six hundred and forty cars. Thirteen thousand. A mass-market car maker sells that many in a few days. And its shipments were basically flat on the year before.

SOFIA: So it's tiny.

MARCO: Tiny in volume. Now the money. Revenue of about seven point one billion euros. EBITDA of about two point seven seven billion. That's an EBITDA margin of almost thirty-nine percent. Net profit, one point six billion. And management says its order book covers it through the end of twenty twenty-seven.

SOFIA: Thirty-nine percent margins? For a car company?

MARCO: That's the point. Mass-market car makers typically earn single-digit to low double-digit margins. Ferrari's margins look like a luxury goods house. It deliberately makes fewer cars than people want, so customers wait, and it controls prices. That's how luxury brands behave.

SOFIA: And how does the market value it?

MARCO: At the start of this month, Ferrari traded at about nineteen times EBITDA, on one data provider's figures. The median for the vehicles and parts industry on the same source: about eight times. So Ferrari trades at more than twice a typical car maker.

SOFIA: And is that expensive?

MARCO: Here's the twist. By its own standards, it's cheap-ish. Ferrari's ten-year median is around twenty-three times, so it's currently below its usual level. Investors have become less sure about how fast it can keep growing. But it's still priced like a luxury business, not a car maker. And whether that's justified depends entirely on which companies you compare it with. That's the art of comps.

SOFIA: Okay. So how does a comps analysis work, step by step?

MARCO: Four steps. One: choose a set of comparable listed companies. Two: gather their financials and calculate their multiples. Bankers call this spreading the comps. Three: look at the range, usually the median, and the twenty-fifth and seventy-fifth percentiles. Four: apply those multiples to your target's metrics to get a valuation range.

SOFIA: Let's do it with numbers.

MARCO: Our café chain. EBITDA of one hundred. We find five listed café and restaurant chains. Their E-V over EBITDA multiples are seven, eight, nine, ten and fifteen.

SOFIA: The average is... seven plus eight plus nine plus ten plus fifteen is forty-nine, over five. Nine point eight. So the café chain is worth nine hundred and eighty.

MARCO: That's the first trap. Look at the fifteen. One company is pulling the average up. Maybe it's growing much faster, maybe it's a takeover target. That's why bankers prefer the median. The median here is nine. And the middle range, from the twenty-fifth to the seventy-fifth percentile, is roughly eight to ten.

SOFIA: So enterprise value between eight hundred and one thousand, with nine hundred in the middle.

MARCO: Right. Then the bridge back to equity value, from episode nine: subtract net debt and so on. A valuation is always a range, not a single number.

SOFIA: Okay. And choosing the peers. You just pick companies in the same industry, right? Ferrari is a car company, so you compare it with car companies.

MARCO: Second trap, and the Ferrari question is exactly why. If you pick peers by the industry label alone, you'd compare Ferrari with Stellantis, Volkswagen, BMW. You'd apply eight times EBITDA to Ferrari and conclude it's massively overvalued. But those companies have completely different margins, growth and pricing power.

SOFIA: So you'd compare it with luxury brands instead. Hermès, L-V-M-H, Moncler.

MARCO: Many analysts do exactly that, or use a mix of luxury and premium auto names. And there's no single right answer. What matters is that you choose peers based on business characteristics, not the label: growth rate, margins, size, geography, business model, risk. Then you defend the choice. In an interview, how you justify the peer set matters more than the exact list.

SOFIA: Is there a rule for how many?

MARCO: Usually somewhere between five and fifteen. Fewer, and one odd company distorts everything. More, and you're including companies that aren't really comparable.

SOFIA: Okay. And comps tell you what the company is worth if someone buys it?

MARCO: Third trap. No. Comps are based on share prices: what investors pay for small stakes in companies trading normally on the stock market. They don't include a control premium, the extra a buyer pays to take control of the whole company. That premium is usually somewhere around twenty to forty percent. So in a takeover, comps tend to give you a lower value than precedent transactions, which do include the premium. That's tomorrow's episode.

SOFIA: Anything else that goes wrong?

MARCO: Two technical things. First, timing. Companies have different financial years. One ends in December, another in March. Bankers adjust them to the same calendar period, which is called calendarisation. Second, consistency. Use the same definitions for every company: same EBITDA adjustments, same treatment of leases, and either all trailing or all forward multiples.

SOFIA: And the big weakness of comps?

MARCO: They're relative. They tell you what a company is worth compared with others, not what it's worth in absolute terms. If the whole sector is overpriced, comps will tell you an overpriced company is fairly priced. In two thousand, internet companies looked reasonable compared with each other. That's why bankers always check comps against a D-C-F.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: walk me through a comparable companies analysis.

SOFIA: You pick similar companies, calculate their multiples, take the median and apply it to the company you're valuing.

MARCO: Good shape. Make it complete. Select a peer group based on industry, size, growth, margins and geography. Gather their financials and calculate multiples such as E-V over EBITDA and P-E, usually on both trailing and forward numbers. Look at the median and the interquartile range. Apply those to the target's metrics to get an implied valuation range, then bridge from enterprise value to equity value and per share.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: How do you choose comparable companies?

SOFIA: Same industry, similar size, similar growth.

MARCO: Then add the insight. The goal is similar risk and growth profiles, not just the same industry label. And give an example: for Ferrari, a peer set of mass-market car makers would undervalue it, because its margins and pricing power are closer to luxury goods.

SOFIA: Third?

MARCO: What are the advantages and disadvantages of comps?

SOFIA: Advantages: it's based on real market prices and it's quick. Disadvantages: there may not be truly comparable companies, and if the market is wrong, the valuation is wrong too.

MARCO: Very good. And add the control premium point: comps reflect minority stakes, so they don't capture what a buyer pays for control. That's a complete answer.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: This week, Fresenius, the German healthcare group, agreed and completed the purchase of the remaining forty-five percent of mAbxience, a company that develops and manufactures biosimilars. Those are near-copies of biological drugs, like antibody treatments, once the original patent expires.

SOFIA: Remaining forty-five percent. So Fresenius already owned fifty-five.

MARCO: Since twenty twenty-two. Now it pays up to seven hundred and fifty million euros for the rest: seven hundred million upfront, plus fifty million if certain manufacturing sites get regulatory approvals.

SOFIA: So the whole company is worth... seven hundred and fifty divided by forty-five percent. About one point six seven billion.

MARCO: Right. And mAbxience had revenue of over three hundred and twenty million euros last year. So roughly five times revenue for the whole business.

SOFIA: And the interview question?

MARCO: Connect it to episode nine. Before this deal, Fresenius owned fifty-five percent and controlled mAbxience. How did it show up in Fresenius's accounts, and what changes now?

SOFIA: Fresenius consolidated one hundred percent of mAbxience's revenue and EBITDA, with a non-controlling interest for the forty-five percent it didn't own. Now that it owns everything, the non-controlling interest disappears.

MARCO: Exactly. And the cash it paid effectively replaces that minority claim. So all of mAbxience's profit now belongs to Fresenius shareholders. That's a strong answer.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: five peers trade at six, eight, nine, eleven and twenty times EBITDA. What's the median? Question two: using that median, your target has EBITDA of fifty and net debt of one hundred. What's its implied equity value? Question three: does a comps valuation include a control premium? Question four: Ferrari trades at about nineteen times EBITDA and the vehicles industry median is about eight. Roughly how many times higher is Ferrari's multiple? Question five: why can mass-market car makers be the wrong peer group for Ferrari?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: nine. Two: nine times fifty is four hundred and fifty of enterprise value, minus one hundred of net debt, three hundred and fifty of equity value. Three: no. Comps are based on minority share prices. Precedent transactions include the control premium. Four: a bit more than double, around two point three times. Five: because Ferrari's margins, pricing power and order book look much more like a luxury goods business than a volume car maker, so their multiples would undervalue it.

SOFIA: I'll never look at a Ferrari the same way.

MARCO: You'll look at it as a luxury brand that happens to have wheels. Tomorrow: precedent transactions, and how much buyers really pay for control.
