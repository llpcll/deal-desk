# The Deal Desk, episode 05: Linking the three statements, and the ten euros of depreciation
The most common technical question in banking interviews, answered cleanly, plus the two variations they use to catch you.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode five. Three days, three statements. Today we wire them together. And we'll finish with the one question you're almost guaranteed to get in a technical interview.

SOFIA: Depreciation goes up by ten.

MARCO: Depreciation goes up by ten. Walk me through the three statements. Mergers and Inquisitions, the site that publishes the famous four hundred questions guide, lists it among the most common accounting questions there is. When I was interviewing candidates, I'd use it as the opener, because in sixty seconds it tells you whether someone actually understands accounting or has just memorised definitions.

SOFIA: And people get it wrong?

MARCO: All the time. Usually in one of three ways. They forget tax. They say cash goes down. Or they get to the balance sheet and it doesn't balance, and they freeze. So first the wiring, then the question.

SOFIA: Okay. Where do the statements connect?

MARCO: Start with net income. It's the bottom line of the income statement. It's also the top line of the cash flow statement. That's link number one.

SOFIA: And link two?

MARCO: Net income also flows into the balance sheet, into retained earnings, inside equity. Retained earnings goes up by net income, and down by any dividends paid. That's how the company's profits build up in equity over time.

SOFIA: Okay. Link three?

MARCO: The cash flow statement ends with the change in cash for the period. Add that to the starting cash balance and you get the ending cash on the balance sheet. That's the link that makes everything reconcile.

SOFIA: And the other lines in the cash flow statement?

MARCO: Every one of them is a change in something on the balance sheet. Depreciation reduces the value of equipment, P-P-and-E, property, plant and equipment. Capex increases it. Changes in receivables, inventory and payables come from working capital on the balance sheet. Borrowing or repaying debt changes the debt line. Issuing shares changes equity. The cash flow statement is basically the story of how the balance sheet changed.

SOFIA: Okay. So, the question. Depreciation goes up by ten.

MARCO: Go ahead. Try it first. Assume a twenty-five percent tax rate.

SOFIA: Income statement: depreciation is an expense, so operating income goes down by ten. And net income goes down by ten.

MARCO: Stop. That's trap number one. What happens between operating income and net income?

SOFIA: Oh. Tax. Pre-tax income is down ten, so tax is down... two point five. Net income is down seven point five.

MARCO: Good. Depreciation is tax-deductible, so the government effectively absorbs a quarter of it. Now the cash flow statement.

SOFIA: Net income is down seven point five. And cash is down ten, because depreciation went up.

MARCO: Trap number two. That's the one I heard most. Depreciation isn't a cash expense. Remember episode two. So on the cash flow statement: net income, down seven point five. Then you add back the depreciation, because it wasn't cash. Plus ten. Cash from operations, up two point five.

SOFIA: Up? Cash goes up because depreciation went up?

MARCO: Yes, by two point five. And be precise about why. Not because depreciation creates cash. Because the higher expense lowered the tax bill by two point five. That's the only real cash effect. People call it the depreciation tax shield.

SOFIA: Okay. So cash at the end is up two point five.

MARCO: Right. Now the balance sheet. Assets first.

SOFIA: Cash up two point five. And... P-P-and-E?

MARCO: Down ten. More depreciation means the equipment is written down by ten more. So total assets: plus two point five, minus ten, down seven point five.

SOFIA: And liabilities and equity. Liabilities don't change. Retained earnings goes down by net income, so down seven point five. Equity down seven point five.

MARCO: Assets down seven point five, liabilities and equity down seven point five. It balances. That's the whole answer, and in an interview you say it in about thirty seconds.

SOFIA: Hang on. Shouldn't the lower tax show up on the balance sheet as a liability? Taxes owed going down?

MARCO: Good question, and it shows you're thinking. In the simple version, we assume tax is paid in cash during the year, so the saving shows up in cash. In real life there can be deferred taxes, where tax accounting and book accounting differ. Interviewers at spring week level almost never go there. If they do, say you're assuming taxes are paid in cash, and that you'd mention deferred taxes as a refinement.

SOFIA: Okay. Let me try saying the whole thing.

MARCO: Go.

SOFIA: Income statement: operating income down ten, tax down two point five, net income down seven point five. Cash flow statement: net income down seven point five, add back ten of depreciation, cash up two point five. Balance sheet: cash up two point five, P-P-and-E down ten, so assets down seven point five. Retained earnings down seven point five, so equity down seven point five. Balances.

MARCO: That's it. Now the variations, because once you nail the standard one, a good interviewer changes a detail to see if you actually understand it.

SOFIA: Like what?

MARCO: Variation one. A company buys a hundred of equipment with cash. Walk me through it.

SOFIA: Income statement: expense of a hundred...

MARCO: Trap number three. Buying equipment isn't an expense. It's an investment in an asset. The expense comes later, spread out as depreciation. So on day one, the income statement: nothing.

SOFIA: Okay. Cash flow statement: investing activities, capex, minus a hundred. Balance sheet: cash down a hundred, P-P-and-E up a hundred. Assets unchanged in total.

MARCO: Perfect. Nothing on liabilities or equity. You swapped one asset for another.

SOFIA: Variation two?

MARCO: The café invoices a client twenty of catering, and the client hasn't paid yet. Tax rate twenty-five percent again.

SOFIA: Income statement: revenue up twenty. Assuming no costs, pre-tax income up twenty, tax up five, net income up fifteen.

MARCO: Keep going.

SOFIA: Cash flow statement: net income up fifteen. Then receivables went up by twenty, and an increase in an asset uses cash. So minus twenty. Cash from operations down five.

MARCO: Down five. Why?

SOFIA: Because we haven't collected anything, but we still owe tax on the profit.

MARCO: Exactly. That's the kind of sentence that impresses an interviewer. Balance sheet?

SOFIA: Cash down five, receivables up twenty. Assets up fifteen. Retained earnings up fifteen. Equity up fifteen. Balances.

MARCO: That's how you do it. And the general method, for any version of the question: start with the income statement, then the cash flow statement starting from net income, then the balance sheet, and always check the two sides match. If they don't, go back and look for tax or a working capital change.

SOFIA: What if the thing doesn't touch the income statement at all? Like borrowing money?

MARCO: Then you just skip it. Borrowing a hundred: nothing on the income statement, plus a hundred in financing, cash up a hundred, debt up a hundred. Interest only starts to appear later, when you pay it. Always ask yourself first: is this an expense or revenue in this period? If not, the income statement doesn't move.

SOFIA: Okay. So what else would they ask on this topic?

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: how are the three financial statements linked?

SOFIA: Net income goes to the cash flow statement and to retained earnings, and cash at the end of the cash flow statement goes to the balance sheet.

MARCO: That's actually a good answer. Add one sentence: the other items on the cash flow statement reflect changes in balance sheet items, such as depreciation reducing P-P-and-E, capex increasing it, and working capital and debt changes. That's complete.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: If depreciation is a non-cash expense, why does it affect cash at all?

SOFIA: Because of tax.

MARCO: Right, now make it a full sentence. Depreciation reduces taxable income, so the company pays less tax, and that tax saving is a real cash benefit. The depreciation itself doesn't move cash.

SOFIA: And third?

MARCO: Third is the curveball. Depreciation goes up by ten, but assume there are no taxes at all. What's the impact on cash?

SOFIA: Zero. Net income down ten, add back ten.

MARCO: Right. Which proves the point. Without tax, depreciation has no cash effect whatsoever.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: This week the British competition regulator, the C-M-A, opened a first look at Kone buying TK Elevator. Kone is a Finnish lift and escalator maker. TK Elevator is the German one, formerly part of thyssenkrupp. The deal was announced in April, and values TK Elevator at about twenty-nine point four billion euros. It would create the world's biggest lift maker by sales, and it's the biggest deal in Finnish history.

SOFIA: Who's selling?

MARCO: Advent International and Cinven, two private equity firms. They bought it from thyssenkrupp in twenty twenty for seventeen point two billion euros.

SOFIA: And how is Kone paying?

MARCO: This is the part to connect with today. About five billion euros in cash, roughly fifteen billion in new Kone shares, and Kone takes on TK Elevator's debt.

SOFIA: So on Kone's balance sheet...

MARCO: Go on. Walk me through it, in broad strokes.

SOFIA: Cash goes down five billion. Equity goes up fifteen billion from the new shares. Debt goes up, because they take on TK Elevator's debt. And on the asset side, they add TK Elevator's assets.

MARCO: That's the right shape. And whatever Kone pays above the value of TK Elevator's identifiable assets goes on the balance sheet as goodwill. We'll do that properly in week four. Your interview question: why would Kone pay mostly in shares rather than cash?

SOFIA: Because it doesn't have enough cash?

MARCO: Partly. A strong answer adds: paying in shares avoids loading Kone with debt, and the sellers keep a stake in the combined company, so they share in the synergies. The downside is dilution for Kone's existing shareholders. And the regulators are the big risk now. When the two biggest players in a market combine, competition authorities in the U-K, Europe and the United States all want a look.

SOFIA: Quiz?

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Tax rate twenty-five percent throughout. Question one: depreciation goes up by twenty. What happens to net income, and to cash? Question two: a company borrows fifty in cash. What happens on each statement? Question three: it pays ten of dividends. Where does that show up? Question four: it buys thirty of inventory with cash. What happens to cash from operations? Question five: where does net income end up on the balance sheet?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: net income down fifteen, cash up five. Two: income statement, nothing. Cash flow, plus fifty in financing. Balance sheet, cash up fifty and debt up fifty. Three: not on the income statement. Minus ten in financing, cash down ten, retained earnings down ten. Four: cash from operations down thirty, because inventory is an asset that went up. Inventory up thirty, cash down thirty on the balance sheet. Five: retained earnings, inside equity.

SOFIA: I got them all. Is that a first?

MARCO: It is. Enjoy it. Tomorrow: working capital, and how Amazon turned it into a machine that lends Amazon money for free.
