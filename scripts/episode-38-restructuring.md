# The Deal Desk, episode 38: Restructuring, or why Hertz's shareholders got paid after all
In twenty twenty Hertz went bankrupt owing billions, and its shareholders, who should have got nothing, came away with more than a billion dollars. Then Konecta, the Madrid call-centre group that merged with Italy's Comdata, asks its lenders for time.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode thirty-eight. Yesterday Mobico showed you a company carrying more debt than it wanted. Today, a company that can't pay its debts at all. Restructuring: what happens next, and who gets what. We start with a bankruptcy that seemed to break the rules.

SOFIA: Which one?

MARCO: Hertz, the American car rental company. In the spring of twenty twenty the pandemic stopped travel, and nobody was renting cars. Hertz had financed its fleet mostly with loans secured on the cars themselves, and at the end of twenty nineteen its total debt was about seventeen billion dollars. On the twenty-second of May twenty twenty, it filed for Chapter Eleven.

SOFIA: The American court process from episode thirty-three. Reorganise, not close.

MARCO: Right. Then something strange happened. The shares fell to a low of fifty-nine cents, and then small investors piled in and pushed them to five dollars fifty, more than nine times the low.

SOFIA: For a bankrupt company?

MARCO: And Hertz tried to take advantage. In June twenty twenty it set out to sell up to a billion dollars of new shares, while warning in its own documents that they could ultimately be worthless. It sold twenty-nine million dollars' worth before the S-E-C, the American markets regulator, stopped it.

SOFIA: Because in a bankruptcy the shareholders get nothing.

MARCO: Usually. But in June twenty twenty-one the court confirmed Hertz's plan, and the plan paid every creditor in full, in cash. More than ninety-seven percent of the shareholders who voted approved it, and they received more than a billion dollars of value: one dollar fifty-three a share in cash, plus a small stake in the new company. Hertz came out of Chapter Eleven on the thirtieth of June twenty twenty-one, with investors led by Knighthead, Certares and Apollo putting in about two point eight billion dollars of new shares.

SOFIA: So the small investors were right.

MARCO: Mostly lucky. The business turned out to be worth more than everything it owed, so once every creditor was paid, something was left for the shareholders. That's the lesson. In a restructuring nobody asks who deserves what. They ask what the business is worth, and where that value runs out in the queue.

SOFIA: So where do we start?

MARCO: With two kinds of trouble. A company can be short of cash: a bond is due next month and nobody will refinance it, but the business is worth more than its debts. That's a liquidity problem. Or the business is worth less than its debts, however long you wait. That's a solvency problem. The first you fix with time. With the second, someone has to take a loss.

SOFIA: And then it goes to court.

MARCO: First trap. Court is slow, expensive and public, so most restructurings start outside it. The company asks its lenders to waive a covenant, or to push the maturities out, which bankers call amend and extend. It asks them to hold off on their rights while everyone talks: that's a forbearance agreement. Or lenders swap part of their debt for shares. Court comes in when not everyone agrees. In America, Chapter Eleven. In Britain, a scheme of arrangement or a restructuring plan, where a class of creditors is bound once seventy-five percent of it by value, of those voting, says yes.

SOFIA: And if a whole class says no?

MARCO: A restructuring plan can still bind it, if that class is no worse off than in the realistic alternative, and a class with a real stake has voted for it. That's a cross-class cram down. Now, who gets what. Round numbers. Northgate makes one hundred million euros of EBITDA. It has borrowed three hundred and fifty million: two hundred of senior loans, secured on its assets, and one hundred and fifty of junior bonds behind them. Then its biggest customer leaves, and EBITDA halves to fifty. The bonds fall due next year, and nobody will refinance them. Similar companies are worth about six times EBITDA. What's Northgate worth?

SOFIA: Six times fifty. Three hundred million.

MARCO: And it owes three hundred and fifty. Who gets the three hundred?

SOFIA: Everyone takes the same haircut. Three hundred over three hundred and fifty, so about eighty-six cents on the euro each.

MARCO: Second trap. Debt isn't one pile, it's a queue. The senior lenders come first. They're owed two hundred, so they get all two hundred: a hundred cents on the euro. That leaves one hundred for the junior bonds, which are owed one hundred and fifty. Two thirds, sixty-seven cents. And the shareholders?

SOFIA: Nothing.

MARCO: Nothing. That's the absolute priority rule: each class is paid in full before the one behind it gets anything. The class where the value runs out, here the junior bonds, has a name: the fulcrum security. Its holders usually become the new owners. The seniors keep their loans, and the juniors swap their one hundred and fifty of bonds for the shares. Afterwards Northgate has two hundred of debt against fifty of EBITDA, four times instead of seven. And the old bondholders own equity worth three hundred minus two hundred: one hundred. The same sixty-seven cents, paid in shares instead of cash.

SOFIA: Why not just close it and sell everything?

MARCO: Because a forced sale fetches far less. Say the buildings and machines raise one hundred and fifty. The seniors get seventy-five cents and the juniors nothing. Keeping the business alive is worth more to almost everyone, and that's why the test for a cram down is the alternative. You can see the queue at work in Britain right now. Under terms that most of Thames Water's Class B creditors signed up to on the sixteenth of October, about one billion pounds of Class B debt would be written off in full, in exchange for shares giving a recovery of about five point nine percent.

SOFIA: Six pence in the pound. So the rest is just arithmetic.

MARCO: Third trap. The arithmetic is easy. The number six isn't. Argue five times: two hundred and fifty, and the juniors get fifty, thirty-three cents. The juniors argue eight times: four hundred. Now they're paid in full, and fifty is left for the old shareholders. That's Hertz. Every class argues for the value that puts the break just below itself, and most of a restructuring is that fight.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: a company has a bond due next year that it can't refinance. What are its options?

SOFIA: It files for bankruptcy, and a court decides who gets paid.

MARCO: That's the last step, not the first. Strong answer: first, is it a liquidity problem or a solvency problem? If the business is worth more than the debt, buy time: sell an asset, raise equity, or amend and extend with the lenders. If it's worth less, someone takes a loss, usually through a debt for equity swap with the fulcrum class. Court when a minority holds out. Liquidation only when the business is worth more in pieces than alive.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: You're a distressed debt investor. Northgate's junior bonds trade at forty cents. Do you buy?

SOFIA: Yes. You pay forty and get back a hundred.

MARCO: Not in a restructuring. Strong answer: value the business, run the waterfall, and compare the recovery with the price. At six times, the juniors recover sixty-seven cents, so forty looks cheap. At five times, thirty-three, and you lose. It's yesterday's markets pitch: what's priced in, where you differ, and what you lose if you're wrong.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A European company at the very first stage. Konecta, based in Madrid, runs customer service and digital outsourcing: call centres, at heart. One hundred and nine thousand employees in twenty-eight countries, and about two billion euros of revenue. It's controlled by Intermediate Capital Group, I-C-G, the British investment firm, which bought it in twenty nineteen from Banco Santander, the fund P-A-I and José María Pacheco, and then merged it with Comdata, an Italian company in the same business.

SOFIA: A buyout, with debt.

MARCO: According to the Spanish press, a loan of nine hundred and forty-five million euros due in October twenty twenty-nine, and a revolving credit facility of two hundred million due in April twenty twenty-nine. In July the loan was trading at about fifty-seven cents on the euro, and the company's earnings covered its interest only one point seven times.

SOFIA: So the market expects the lenders to lose forty-three percent.

MARCO: Fourth trap. That's a price, not a recovery. A buyer of distressed debt wants a high return for the risk, so it pays well below what it expects to get back. Fifty-seven tells you the market doubts full repayment. It doesn't tell you the loss.

SOFIA: And what happened this week?

MARCO: On Monday the twenty-sixth of October, Konecta said it was in constructive talks with its shareholders and a majority of the lenders under its main loan, to recapitalise the group and strengthen its liquidity. Then on Friday the thirtieth of October, it announced a forbearance agreement with a majority of those lenders, so it can keep running its transformation plan while the talks go on.

SOFIA: So the lenders agree to wait.

MARCO: They agree not to enforce their rights for a while, so nobody pulls the trigger mid-negotiation. Konecta hasn't published the terms, the length, or how much debt might be cut, so I won't guess. But you can name the questions. Where does the value break: inside the loan, or above it? Does I-C-G put in new money to keep a stake, or do the lenders take the keys? And notice the company's message. On Monday its chief executive said the digital business was growing at nearly thirty percent a year.

SOFIA: That's the juniors arguing for eight times.

MARCO: Exactly. Value the business higher, and the break moves down the queue, towards the shareholders. That's the argument to watch.

MARCO: Quiz. For you listening. Question one: Northgate is worth three hundred million euros. It owes two hundred of senior loans and one hundred and fifty of junior bonds. How much do the junior bonds recover? Question two: what is the fulcrum security? Question three: a company is worth more than its debts, but can't refinance a bond due next month. What kind of problem is that? Question four: why did Hertz's shareholders get anything at all? Question five: Konecta has signed a forbearance agreement. What does that mean?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: sixty-seven cents on the euro. The seniors take their two hundred first, and one hundred is left for one hundred and fifty of bonds. Eighty-six cents is the equal haircut, and that's not how the queue works. Two: the class where the value runs out. Its holders usually swap their debt for the shares and become the owners. Three: a liquidity problem, fixed with time, not a solvency problem, where someone takes a loss. Four: the business was worth more than everything it owed, so every creditor was paid in full and value was left over. Five: the lenders agree not to enforce their rights for a while, so the talks can go on.

SOFIA: So, what the business is worth, where the value runs out, and who's next in the queue.

MARCO: And the arguments about value are where the work is. Tomorrow, episode thirty-nine: fit questions. Why banking, why this bank, and the stories behind your C-V.
