# The Deal Desk, episode 17: Terminal value, or the judge who changed one number
A Delaware court moved one assumption from three point one percent to four. The Supreme Court sent the valuation back.

---

MARCO: This is The Deal Desk, episode seventeen. Yesterday we set the discount rate. Today: what is a business worth after the forecast stops? Terminal value. And we start, once again, in a Delaware courtroom.

SOFIA: Not Dell again.

MARCO: Not Dell. A smaller case, with a sharper lesson. D-F-C Global, a payday lender, the kind of company that makes short, expensive loans to people between pay cheques. After the company was shopped around for two years, the private equity firm Lone Star, the only bidder left at the end, bought it for nine dollars fifty a share.

SOFIA: And some shareholders said that was too low.

MARCO: They did, and they asked the Court of Chancery for an appraisal. The court built its own D-C-F and got thirteen dollars and seven cents a share. Then it weighed three numbers equally: the deal price, a comparable companies value of eight dollars and seven cents, and its own D-C-F. Fair value: ten dollars twenty-one.

SOFIA: Where's the twist?

MARCO: The company pointed out a mistake in the court's working capital figures. Fixing it would have pushed the D-C-F below the deal price. But the shareholders argued that the corrected figures implied faster long-term growth. So the court changed one number, the rate at which the business grows forever, from three point one percent to four. The D-C-F went back above the deal price, and fair value came out at ten dollars thirty.

SOFIA: One assumption, and the answer flips.

MARCO: And in August twenty seventeen, the Delaware Supreme Court reversed the decision. In its words, no adequate basis in the record supports this major change in growth rate. The projections were already optimistic, and the industry had already had its years of fast growth.

SOFIA: Less than one point of growth. Why does that matter so much?

MARCO: Because of where it sits. Most of a D-C-F's value is in the terminal value, the value of everything after the forecast. By the end of today you'll be able to show why nine tenths of a point can move a court.

SOFIA: Okay. So the forecast runs five years. Why not just forecast fifty?

MARCO: You could, but by year fifty you're guessing. So we forecast in detail while we can see the business, usually five to ten years, then capture everything after that in one number. Two ways to do it. The first is the perpetuity growth method, also called the Gordon growth model. Assume the cash flow grows at a steady rate forever. Then terminal value equals next year's cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate.

SOFIA: Forever? That sounds like infinity.

MARCO: It would be, without discounting. Each year further out is worth less today, so the sum settles on a finite number. Take Cafe Group. Its discount rate is nine percent, from yesterday. Say its unlevered free cash flow in year five, the last forecast year, is one hundred, and after that it grows at two percent a year.

SOFIA: So one hundred divided by nine minus two. One hundred over seven percent. About one thousand four hundred and thirty.

MARCO: First trap. The formula wants next year's cash flow, the first year after the forecast. One hundred grown by two percent is one hundred and two. Divided by seven percent, about one thousand four hundred and sixty.

SOFIA: So I add one thousand four hundred and sixty to the forecast years, and I'm done.

MARCO: Second trap. Terminal value is a value at the end of year five. It's money you'd only see five years from now, so you discount it back five years like any other cash flow. At nine percent, divide by about one point five four. Roughly nine hundred and fifty today.

SOFIA: So it loses about a third.

MARCO: And it's still the biggest piece. Say the five forecast years add up to just under four hundred in today's money. Add the nine hundred and fifty. Enterprise value about one thousand three hundred and forty, and about seventy percent of it comes from the terminal value.

SOFIA: Then the growth rate matters a lot. Cafe Group is growing eight percent a year at the moment. Why not use eight?

MARCO: Third trap, and it's D-F-C's trap. Nothing grows faster than the economy forever, or one day it would be the economy. The long-term rate should sit at or below the long-run growth of the economy, inflation included. Usually two to three percent in developed markets. Watch what eight does. One hundred and eight, divided by nine minus eight, one percent. Ten thousand eight hundred.

SOFIA: More than seven times as much.

MARCO: And at nine percent growth you'd divide by zero. The formula breaks, because growth has caught up with the discount rate. Even small moves hurt. Take growth from two percent to three: one hundred and three over six percent, about one thousand seven hundred and twenty. Eighteen percent more terminal value for one point of growth. That's D-F-C's courtroom.

SOFIA: And the second method?

MARCO: The exit multiple method. Assume the business is sold at the end of the forecast, at a multiple like the ones comparable companies trade at. Say Cafe Group's EBITDA in year five is one hundred and fifty, and similar café chains trade at eight times EBITDA. Terminal value: one thousand two hundred.

SOFIA: That's better. It's based on real prices. No guessing about forever.

MARCO: Fourth trap. A multiple is a growth assumption in disguise. Whoever pays eight times in year five is paying for the growth after year five. And you're using today's multiples for a sale years away. Remember the stale eighteen times deal from twenty twenty-one in episode twelve. Pick the wrong moment and your terminal value inherits the whole mood of the market.

SOFIA: So which one do bankers use?

MARCO: Both, and they check one against the other. Our growth method gave about one thousand four hundred and sixty, which is nearly ten times year-five EBITDA. The comparable companies say eight. So either two percent growth is too generous, or eight times is too cautious. That gap is a question to answer, not a number to average away.

SOFIA: Okay.

MARCO: What they'll ask you. First: what are the two ways to calculate terminal value, and which do you prefer?

SOFIA: Perpetuity growth and exit multiple. I prefer the exit multiple, because it's based on the market.

MARCO: Half right. Name both with their formulas. Perpetuity growth: next year's cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate. Exit multiple: final-year EBITDA times a multiple from comparable companies. The first rests on the business's own cash, the second on what the market pays today. Use one, and cross-check it with the implied multiple or the implied growth rate.

SOFIA: Second?

MARCO: Terminal value is seventy-five percent of your D-C-F. Is something wrong?

SOFIA: Yes. The model depends too much on the far future, so I'd shorten the forecast.

MARCO: No, and a shorter forecast would push even more value into the terminal value. More than half is normal for a healthy company, as we said in episode thirteen. What it tells you is where to spend your time: test the growth rate, the discount rate and the multiple, and show the answer as a range. That's tomorrow's episode.

SOFIA: Deal of the day?

MARCO: A French buyer, and a price where the terminal value does a lot of the work. On Monday, the fifth of October, Schneider Electric, the French energy management and automation group, agreed to buy P-T-C, the American industrial software company, for two hundred and five dollars a share in cash.

SOFIA: How big?

MARCO: About twenty-two point six billion dollars for the shares, and an enterprise value of twenty-three point seven billion dollars, or twenty-one point one billion euros. The price is a premium of forty-two point three percent to P-T-C's last close, one hundred and forty-four dollars and three cents on the Friday.

SOFIA: And the multiple?

MARCO: Schneider puts it at twenty-one times P-T-C's expected adjusted EBITA for twenty twenty-seven. That's operating profit before amortisation. Including all the savings it expects, thirteen times. Those are cost savings of two hundred and fifty million euros a year by the third year, plus about eight hundred million euros of extra revenue from selling more together.

SOFIA: How is it paid for?

MARCO: In cash, backed by a bridge loan from Morgan Stanley and Société Générale. Schneider then plans to issue about five to six billion euros of new shares and sixteen to seventeen billion euros of new debt. Completion is expected by the third quarter of twenty twenty-seven, subject to P-T-C's shareholders and the regulators.

SOFIA: Twenty-one times sounds expensive.

MARCO: For a business standing still, it would be. In twenty twenty-five P-T-C had revenue of two point four billion euros, with an adjusted EBITA margin of about forty percent, and Schneider expects its revenue and recurring revenue to grow about ten percent a year to twenty twenty-nine. So here's your interview question. You're building a D-C-F of P-T-C with a five-year forecast. What growth rate goes into the terminal value?

SOFIA: Ten percent. That's what Schneider expects.

MARCO: That's D-F-C's mistake on a much bigger stage. Ten percent forever is far above the economy, and close enough to any discount rate to send the value off the chart. The strong answer: ten percent belongs in the forecast years, while the business is growing fast. Then either extend the forecast until growth fades towards the economy's rate, and only then apply the formula, or use an exit multiple and check what growth it implies. And the savings go into the terminal value too, so whether they arrive, and last, decides whether Schneider paid a fair price.

SOFIA: So the price depends mostly on what happens after twenty twenty-nine.

MARCO: Mostly, yes. Quiz. For you listening. Question one: the final-year free cash flow is two hundred, long-term growth is two percent, and the discount rate is ten percent. What's the terminal value with the perpetuity growth method? Question two: you've calculated a terminal value at the end of year five. What do you do with it before adding it to the value of the forecast years? Question three: why shouldn't the perpetuity growth rate be above the long-run growth rate of the economy? Question four: terminal value is seventy-five percent of your D-C-F value. What do you do? Question five: P-T-C's revenue is expected to grow about ten percent a year to twenty twenty-nine. How do you handle that in a D-C-F?

MARCO: Pause here if you want to think it through.

MARCO: Answers. One: two thousand five hundred and fifty. Two hundred grown by two percent is two hundred and four, divided by ten minus two, eight percent. Two: discount it back five years, at the same rate as the forecast cash flows. Three: because a company growing faster than the economy forever would end up bigger than the economy itself. Two to three percent is the usual range. Four: nothing is broken; that's normal. Test the assumptions behind it and show a range. Five: keep the ten percent in the forecast years, let growth fade before the terminal value, and cross-check with an exit multiple.

SOFIA: So the forecast is the part you can see, and the terminal value is the part you pay for.

MARCO: Nicely put. And because one assumption can move the whole answer, nobody serious presents a D-C-F as a single number. Tomorrow, episode eighteen: D-C-F sensitivities and traps. The table every banker builds, and the mistakes that make a model lie.
